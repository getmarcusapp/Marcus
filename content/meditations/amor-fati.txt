Amor fati. Love of fate. The phrase is Nietzsche's. The practice is far older. The Stoics taught it nearly two thousand years before he named it.
[[8]]
Epictetus put it in one sentence. Do not seek to have everything that happens happen as you wish, but wish for everything to happen as it actually does happen, and your life will be serene.
[[12]]
Wish for things to happen as they actually do. It sounds like giving up. It is the opposite. It is the end of a war you were never going to win: the war with what has already happened.
[[10]]
Bring to mind something in your life right now that you wish were different. Not the largest thing. Something real, and close.
[[30]]
Notice the wishing. The quiet insistence that it should not be this way. Notice how much effort that takes, every day, and how little it changes.
[[30]]
Now try something. Say to yourself: this is what is happening. Not good, not bad. Just this.
[[25]]
Marcus Aurelius went further. He wrote to the order of things itself: I am in harmony with all, that is a part of thy harmony, great Universe.
[[12]]
He did not mean that every event is pleasant. He meant that every event is part of a whole, and that the whole is not his to rewrite.
[[10]]
Look again at the thing you brought to mind. Ask: given that this is happening, what can I make of it? What does it ask me to become?

The difficulty is the material. A setback can teach patience. A loss can teach what mattered. An obstacle can show you a road you would not have seen.

You do not have to like it. You only have to stop refusing it, so that your strength goes into the response and not the resistance.

What is left when you stop fighting the fact? Often it is quieter than you expected. Often it is clearer.

This is the practice. Not loving every event. Meeting every event as yours, and doing something worthy with it.
