Find a position where your body is supported. Let the ground, or the chair, hold your weight.
[[11]]
This practice is for anxiety. For the moments when the mind runs ahead and does not come back.
[[8]]
Begin by noticing where the fear sits in your body. The chest. The stomach. The throat. You do not have to change it. Just find it.
[[27]]
Now breathe toward it. A slow breath in. A longer breath out. Let the out-breath be the longer one.
[[32]]
Seneca wrote to his friend Lucilius, who was frightened of something that had not yet happened. There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality.
[[13]]
More often in imagination than in reality. The thing you fear may come, or it may not. But the suffering you are feeling now is happening here, in the imagining.
[[11]]
Name the fear to yourself, plainly. Not the whole story. One sentence. I am afraid that this will happen.
[[32]]
Now ask, gently: is it happening now? In this room, in this breath?
[[27]]
Seneca's advice was not to pretend the danger away. It was to look at it clearly. Is it certain? Is it likely? Or only possible?
[[32]]
And if it does come, what would you do? Not how would you feel. What would you do, first?
[[32]]
Notice that you have an answer. Even a small one. A next step. A person to call. A way to begin.
[[13]]
That is the difference between fear and reality. Fear has no next step. Reality always does.
[[16]]
Bring your attention back to the breath. In, and a long breath out.
[[32]]
The future will arrive one moment at a time, and you will meet it one moment at a time. You do not have to meet all of it tonight.
[[16]]
For now, there is only this breath, and this one, and this.
[[32]]
When you are ready, open your eyes. You are here. This is where you live.
