Settle in. Let your eyes close, or let your gaze rest somewhere still.
[[12]]
Listen to your breath. Each one arrives, and each one leaves. None of them stays.
[[20]]
The oldest image in philosophy for this is a river. Heraclitus said it first. You cannot step twice into the same rivers; for fresh waters are ever flowing in upon you.
[[12]]
The river looks the same from the bank. It has a name, a shape, a course. But the water you see now is not the water you saw a moment ago. It is always new, always passing.
[[10]]
Marcus Aurelius returned to this image often. He wrote: Being is as it were a torrent, in and out of which all bodies pass.
[[12]]
Picture that river now. Wide, and steady, and moving. Watch it for a while. Let it carry your attention downstream.
[[35]]
Now let the things of your life appear on its surface. This morning. Yesterday. A worry from last week. Watch each one arrive, pass in front of you, and move on.
[[40]]
Notice that you do not have to hold on to any of them. The river carries them whether you hold or not.
[[20]]
Marcus asked himself a question about this. In this river of existence how can one prize much any of the things that race by, on none of which one can take firm stand?
[[12]]
He did not mean that nothing matters. He meant: do not stake your peace on things that are already moving away.
[[10]]
Now think of something you have been gripping tightly. A plan. A grievance. A fear about how something will turn out. Place it on the water.
[[30]]
Watch it float. Watch it grow smaller. You can still see it. You are just no longer holding it in your hands.
[[35]]
And notice what does not move. The one who is watching. Your attention, here, on the bank. The river passes. You remain to meet whatever comes next.
[[30]]
This is the practice. Not to stop the river. To stand well beside it, and to act well in the moment it brings you.
[[15]]
Take one slow breath. Feel the moment you are in. It is the only part of the river you can touch.
[[20]]
When you are ready, open your eyes.
