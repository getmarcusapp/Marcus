Seneca is the most immediately enjoyable of the three main Stoics and the most confusing to start, because there is so much of him: 124 letters, a dozen essays, three consolations, and a set of blood-soaked tragedies most people never learn he wrote.

Reading the letters in order is the usual mistake. They were not composed as a course, and Letter 1 is not an introduction.

## Start here

**On the Shortness of Life.** Twenty pages, furious, and the best single entry point in Stoic literature. Its argument is that life is not short, we simply waste most of it and then complain, and it is directed with real contempt at people who guard their money and give away their afternoons. If Seneca is going to work for you, you will know by page five.

**Then the letters, selectively.** These are the ones to read first, and they stand alone:

- **Letter 1** — on time as the only thing genuinely ours, and the only thing we let people take
- **Letter 2** — on reading widely being a form of not reading at all
- **Letter 7** — on crowds, and how a day among them leaves you worse
- **Letter 12** — on old age, and going to sleep able to say you have lived
- **Letter 18** — on setting aside days to eat plainly and ask "is this what I feared?"
- **Letter 47** — on treating slaves as human beings. Extraordinary for its date, and one of the reasons Seneca is still read
- **Letter 63** — on grieving for his friend Serenus, and on grieving badly
- **Letter 101** — on living immediately, and counting each day as a separate life

That is a good first month. After it, read straight through from wherever you like; the letters get longer and more technical toward the end, and nobody minds if you stop.

**Then the consolations**, if you want them. *To Marcia* is the fullest, and we discuss the uneven quality of the [Stoic material on grief](/stoicism-and-grief) separately.

## Which edition

**Robin Campbell's *Letters from a Stoic*** (Penguin) is the standard selection and the one we recommend in our [Library](/library). It is a subset rather than the complete set, which for a first encounter is a feature.

**C.D.N. Costa's *On the Shortness of Life*** (Penguin) collects the essay with two others and takes an evening.

**The complete letters** are available in the Chicago translation by Margaret Graver and A.A. Long if you get far enough to want all 124. Most people do not need this and some end up glad they bought it.

Avoid the free public-domain Seneca for the same reason as the free *Meditations*: it is Victorian and the distance you feel will be the translator's. See [which translation of Meditations to read](/best-translation-of-meditations) for the fuller version of that argument.

## The Seneca problem

You will meet this within about ten pages, so it is better to have it in advance.

Seneca wrote about the worthlessness of wealth while being one of the richest men in Rome. He wrote about the tyranny of power while serving as Nero's tutor and then his minister, staying in the role through some of what Nero did and composing at least one public letter defending an act he must have known was indefensible. He was ordered to kill himself and did, with some dignity, which is the part most often recounted.

There are three responses and only one of them is honest.

**Dismiss him as a hypocrite.** Common, satisfying, and it costs you the best prose in Stoicism.

**Defend him.** He restrained Nero for years; the alternative was worse; wealth is a preferred indifferent and a Stoic may possess it. Some of this is true and none of it fully lands.

**Read him as a man writing about what he was failing at.** This is the useful posture, and Seneca half-invites it: he says repeatedly that he is not a sage, that he is a patient in the same ward, that he is describing the treatment rather than his recovery. Read that way the letters become more useful rather than less, because almost nobody reading them is a sage either.

Marcus wrote to himself and it shows. Epictetus taught and it shows. Seneca is defending a position he has not lived up to, and that is the register of most people's actual moral life.

## What he is best on

- **Time**, better than anyone. Letters 1 and 49, and *On the Shortness of Life*
- **Anger**, in three books that are the most practical treatment in the tradition. See [what the Stoics said about anger](/stoicism-and-anger)
- **Death**, constantly and without flinching
- **Friendship**, which the other two barely touch

Where he is weakest is systematic philosophy. He is not building a structure; he is writing letters to a friend, which is exactly why he is readable.
