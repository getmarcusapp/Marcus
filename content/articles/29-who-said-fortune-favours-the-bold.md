This one is not quite a fake, which makes it more interesting than most.

Seneca did write the words down. In one of his letters he quotes them, in Latin, as a familiar verse. But he did not compose them, he was not the first to use them, and his own philosophy of fortune points in nearly the opposite direction.

## Who actually said it

The famous version is Virgil's. In Book X of the *Aeneid*, as Aeneas's fleet arrives to land, the Rutulian king Turnus rallies his men to attack at the water's edge while the enemy is still unsteady on the sand:

> audentis Fortuna iuvat.
>
> — Virgil, *Aeneid* X.284

That is three words, a half-line, and in John Dryden's 1697 translation it becomes "Fortune befriends the bold." The man who says it is the epic's antagonist, and he does not end well.

Virgil did not invent the idea either. It was already proverbial in Latin:

- **Terence**, in his comedy *Phormio* (161 BC), gives a version to a slave named Geta, urging a panicking young man to keep his nerve: *fortis fortuna adiuvat*.
- **Cicero**, in the *Tusculan Disputations* (45 BC), calls it an old proverb outright. In C. D. Yonge's translation: "For not only does Fortune, as the old proverb says, assist the bold, but reason does so in a still greater degree."
- **Pliny the Elder** said it during the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79, according to his nephew's letter to Tacitus. Told to turn back, he answered, in William Melmoth's translation: "Fortune," said he, "favours the brave; steer to where Pomponianus is." He died on that expedition.

So the line belongs to Latin as a whole, made famous by Virgil, and older than any one author.

## Where Seneca comes in

In his *Letters to Lucilius*, Letter 94, Seneca is arguing that short, weighty maxims do real work on us, even without proof. As an example he quotes two lines that "someone repeats to you." One of them is this, in Richard Gummere's translation:

> Fortune favours the brave, but the coward is foiled by his faint heart.
>
> — Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 94.28, trans. Gummere

Gummere's note says the verse is made up from Virgil's line and an unknown author. Seneca is quoting, not composing, and he uses it to make a point about how proverbs work, not to endorse it as his own philosophy. That single appearance is a likely reason his name got attached, though no one has traced the exact moment it happened.

## What Seneca actually thought about fortune

Here is where the attribution goes from imprecise to misleading. Seneca's own view of fortune was not that it rewards the bold. It was that it tests them.

In *On Providence*, he argues that fortune deliberately seeks out the bravest people as its opponents, the way a gladiator wants a worthy adversary: *fortissimos sibi pares quaerit*, it seeks the bravest as its equals. His examples are men who suffered for their courage: Mucius, Regulus, Socrates, Cato. Later in the same essay comes the line that sums it up:

> Fire is the test of gold; adversity, of strong men.
>
> — Seneca, *On Providence* 5

The proverb says boldness is rewarded. Seneca says boldness gets you tested, and that the testing is the point. You can hold either view. They are not the same, and only the second is his.

## What to quote

If you want the proverb, credit Virgil, or call it an old Latin proverb, which Cicero already did.

If you want Seneca, quote *On Providence*. It is the harder thought and the more Stoic one, and it is actually his.
