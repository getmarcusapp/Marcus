Ask most people what a stoic is and you get a man who feels nothing. Jaw set, face still, grief swallowed. It is the dictionary definition, and it has almost nothing to do with the philosophy.

The confusion is baked into English. Lowercase *stoic* means uncomplaining endurance, a usage that drifted away from its source centuries ago. Uppercase *Stoic* means an adherent of a Greek and Roman philosophical school with a detailed theory of the emotions. The two words look identical and mean different things, and the smaller one has largely eaten the larger.

This matters practically, not just pedantically. People arrive at Stoicism believing the goal is to stop feeling, try it, find that suppression makes everything worse, and conclude the philosophy does not work. They were never practicing it.

## What the Stoics actually claimed

The Stoic position is not that emotion is bad. It is that emotion follows from judgment, and that judgment can be examined.

A *pathos*, usually translated as passion, is not a feeling in our sense. It is a feeling produced by a mistaken judgment about what is good or bad. You lose money and you are distraught, because you judged money to be a genuine good. The distress is downstream of the judgment. Correct the judgment and the distress does not need suppressing, because it does not arise in the same way.

*Apatheia*, the goal, is where the damage gets done in translation. It looks like apathy and means something almost opposite: freedom from being ruled by the passions. Not the absence of feeling but the absence of being dragged.

And the Stoics explicitly held that the wise person still feels. They named three good states, the *eupatheiai*:

- **Joy** (*chara*), the rational counterpart to pleasure
- **Caution** (*eulabeia*), the rational counterpart to fear
- **Wish** (*boulesis*), the rational counterpart to appetite

A philosophy aiming at emotional deadness does not maintain a taxonomy of the emotions a sage still experiences.

## The first movements

The sharpest evidence that this was never about suppression is a distinction Seneca draws in *On Anger*, and it is the part of Stoic psychology most worth knowing.

Before anger there is a jolt. Someone insults you and something happens in your chest before you have decided anything: a flush, a quickening, an impulse. Seneca calls these first movements, *propatheiai*, and he is emphatic that they are involuntary, that the wise person has them too, and that they are **not** passions and not faults.

The passion is what happens next, when you assent to the impression, when you agree that you have been wronged and that this is intolerable. That assent is the part that is yours.

So the Stoic is not the person who does not flinch. The Stoic is the person who flinches and does not necessarily proceed from there. This is a much more achievable standard than the popular version, and a much more useful one, because it locates the work at the only point where work is possible.

## Seneca wept

The biographical evidence is not subtle.

Seneca lost his friend Serenus and wrote about grieving badly for him. In Letter 63 he tells Lucilius that his tears fell against his will and that this is permitted, that we should let them flow but not command them, that the man who says a wise person does not grieve is asking for a hardness beyond human. He argues against *excessive* and *performed* grief, and against grief cultivated because we think it is owed. Not against grief.

Marcus Aurelius, who had every incentive to project imperial composure, filled a private notebook with the same three or four consolations repeated for twenty years. You do not rehearse a lesson that often unless it keeps failing to stick. The *Meditations* is not the record of a man who felt nothing. It is the record of a man managing what he felt.

Epictetus is the strictest of the three and even he says: when you see someone in grief, be willing to groan with them, and be careful not to groan inwardly. Note that the outward sympathy is required.

## Why the myth survives

Three reasons, and they compound.

**The word.** English handed us a homonym that means the caricature.

**Marketing.** Stoicism sells better as emotional armor. "Never let them see you sweat" moves more product than "examine the judgments underlying your reactions." Much of the modern popularization has quietly leaned on the harder-sounding version.

**It is easier.** Suppression is a technique you can attempt immediately. Examining your judgments is slow, and it requires being wrong about things you currently believe. Given a choice between an appealing misreading and the demanding original, the misreading spreads.

## What the practice actually asks

Not less feeling. More scrutiny of what produced it.

The Stoic exercises all point at the same place: the moment between an impression arriving and your agreement with it. The morning review sorts what is yours from what is not, so that fewer impressions carry false urgency. The [evening examination](/stoic-evening-examination) audits the judgments you made. [Premeditatio malorum](/premeditatio-malorum) removes the shock that turns difficulty into distress.

None of that involves clamping down on a feeling. It involves getting to the judgment underneath it, which is the only part you can actually change.

If a Stoic practice is making you colder toward the people around you, it has gone wrong. Justice is one of the [four cardinal virtues](/four-stoic-virtues), and Marcus keeps telling himself we were made to work with one another. A version of this philosophy that produces a person who is harder to reach is a version that has lost three quarters of the content.
