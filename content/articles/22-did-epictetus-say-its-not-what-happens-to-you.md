The idea is his. The sentence is not.

"It's not what happens to you, but how you react to it that matters" is a modern paraphrase of *Enchiridion* 5. It is not a translation of it, and no translator has ever produced those words. It circulates in quotation marks with his name beneath, which is the only thing wrong with it, and it turns out to be enough.

## What he actually wrote

*Enchiridion* 5 opens with a claim about where disturbance comes from. Renderings differ, since the Greek has been put into English many times over four centuries, but the sense is consistent:

> People are disturbed not by things, but by the opinions they hold about things.

Then it does something the paraphrase does not do. It gives an example, and then it gives you a place to stand.

The example is death. Death is not itself terrible, Epictetus says, because if it were, it would have looked terrible to Socrates. What is terrible is the opinion that death is terrible. The frightening thing is doing work that you have credited to the event.

And then the passage ends with a progression that is, for my money, the most useful thing in the *Enchiridion*:

> To blame others for your own troubles is the mark of someone who has not begun. To blame yourself is the mark of someone who has begun. To blame neither is the mark of someone who has finished.

None of that survives in eleven words about reacting.

## Why this case is different

Two other entries on [our misattributions list](/misattributed-stoic-quotes) are wrong about who wrote the line. ["What we do in life echoes in eternity"](/did-marcus-aurelius-say-echoes-in-eternity) belongs to a screenwriter, and Marcus argued the reverse. ["We are what we repeatedly do"](/did-aristotle-say-we-are-what-we-repeatedly-do) belongs to Will Durant, summarizing Aristotle accurately.

This one is different again, and it is the harder case, because there is nobody else to name. No rival author, no film, no 1926 bestseller. The thought genuinely is Epictetus's. What went wrong happened at the level of punctuation: a paraphrase acquired quotation marks.

That sounds like the smallest possible offense. It produces the largest drift of the three.

## Reaction is not judgment

Read the two versions next to each other and the substitution is easy to miss.

The paraphrase says the decisive thing is **how you react**. Epictetus says the decisive thing is **the opinion you hold**. Those name different moments, and the gap between them is where his entire method lives.

His model of the mind runs in three steps. An impression arrives, unbidden and uncontrollable. You either assent to it or you do not. Impulse follows from the assent. Reaction is the third step. Judgment is the second. By the time you are reacting, the work that mattered has already been done, and you are managing consequences.

So the paraphrase points at the wrong stage. It reads as advice about composure: something bad has happened, now respond well, stay level, do not lash out. Serviceable counsel, and roughly what most people take Stoicism to be. But Epictetus is not talking about your conduct after the event. He is talking about the belief you attached to it, silently and instantly, in the moment it arrived, which is the belief that made it an event worth reacting to at all.

One is emotional self-management. The other is an argument about what you have quietly assumed to be true. They are not the same discipline, and only the second one explains why he thought philosophy required years rather than resolve.

## The cousin you have also seen

Alongside the paraphrase runs a numbered version: "Life is 10% what happens to you and 90% how you react to it." That one is usually traced to Charles Swindoll, an American pastor, writing in the late twentieth century. It is not Epictetus at all, and the arithmetic is his own.

The two get conflated constantly, which is how a Stoic-sounding sentence and a devotional one end up on the same quote card under the same ancient name.

## So is it wrong to say it?

As a summary, no. If someone asks what Epictetus thought and you say it is not what happens to you but how you respond, you have told them something broadly true and you have not misled them badly.

The failure is the quotation marks. They make a claim of their own: that these are his words, in this order, and that you could go and find them. You cannot. And once a paraphrase is presented as a quotation, nobody has any reason to open the book, because the sentence appears to be the whole of it.

That is the actual cost here, and it is not pedantic. The paraphrase is a closed door with a sign on it saying there is nothing further inside. There is quite a lot further inside.

## What to read instead

The *Enchiridion* is about thirty pages and chapter 5 is one paragraph. You can read the passage this paraphrase came from in under a minute and get the example, the argument, and the progression about blame, none of which fit on a quote card.

Which edition matters more than usual here, because one of the books sold under Epictetus's name is not a translation at all: see [Discourses, Enchiridion, or The Art of Living](/epictetus-discourses-enchiridion-or-art-of-living) before you buy. The editions worth owning are on our [Library](/library) page, and Epictetus himself is on [The Stoics](/stoics).
