No, he did not.

"What we do in life echoes in eternity" is a line from *Gladiator*, the 2000 film, written by David Franzoni, John Logan and William Nicholson. Maximus says it to his cavalry in the woods before the opening battle in Germania. It appears in no manuscript of the *Meditations*, in no translation, in no ancient source at all.

It spread because the film's emperor is Marcus Aurelius. Richard Harris plays him in the first act, the *Meditations* are name-checked in the culture around the film, and a memorable line spoken in that world drifted from the screenplay to the emperor within a few years. By now it is on merchandise, tattoos, and a great many quote cards.

## What makes this one interesting

Most misattributions are merely wrong. This one is wrong in a more specific way: **Marcus argued against the sentiment.**

Posthumous fame is one of his recurring subjects, and he is consistently dismissive of it. He returns to it often enough that it is clearly something he had to keep talking himself out of caring about.

> Those who are eager to be remembered after death do not reflect that everyone who remembers them will very soon die too, and then those who follow, until the whole memory is extinguished as it passes along a line of people who catch fire and then go out.

He notes that names once on everyone's lips are already archaic. He asks what is actually gained by being praised by people you will never meet, most of whom will be wrong about you. In Book IV he says plainly that soon you will have forgotten everything, and soon everything will have forgotten you.

So the line is not just absent from the *Meditations*. It expresses a view the *Meditations* spends considerable effort demolishing. Marcus's position is closer to: what we do in life is forgotten quite quickly, and that is fine, and it is not the reason to do it well.

## Why it matters more than pedantry

His actual argument is more useful than the film's version, and it is the more demanding one.

If the reason to act well is that it echoes in eternity, then the motive is legacy, and legacy is an external. It depends on other people remembering you, which you do not control, and which the *Meditations* keeps pointing out will not happen for long. That makes the whole structure fragile: a person motivated by echo has staked their conduct on something outside their power.

Marcus's version removes the reward entirely. You act well because acting well is the only good available to you, and it remains the only good after everyone who could witness it is gone. Nothing echoes. Do it anyway.

That is a harder sentence to put on a poster, which is roughly why the other one won.

## The film is not the villain

Worth saying: *Gladiator* is not pretending to be a documentary, and screenwriters are entitled to write good lines. The failure is downstream, in a quotation culture that strips context and attaches a famous name because the name and the line were nearby.

That failure is not rare. It is the normal condition of this material. We audited our own quote library and found fifty-five passages that were not what they claimed to be, including this one, which is [why we published the list](/misattributed-stoic-quotes).

## What to read instead

The passages on fame and oblivion are scattered through the *Meditations*, mostly in Books III, IV and VIII. They are short and blunt and much better than the line they are usually replaced by.
