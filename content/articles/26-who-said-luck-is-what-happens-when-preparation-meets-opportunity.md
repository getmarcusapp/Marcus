Seneca did not say it. Nobody has ever produced a letter, essay or play of his that contains it, and the saying has a documented history that starts in America, in print, nearly two thousand years after he died.

The trail below comes mostly from two researchers who did the digging: the etymologist Barry Popik, who compiled the early printed uses in 2011, and a Wikiquote contributor who searched Google Books for the Seneca attribution in 2010. We have not seen every original scan ourselves, so where a date rests on their work, we say so.

## Where it actually comes from

The earliest version found so far is unsigned, in an American magazine for young readers, *The Youth's Companion*, in 1912:

> He is lucky who realizes that "luck" is the point where preparation meets opportunity.

Through the 1920s it turns up in banking. Popik found it in *Bankers Magazine* in 1926 and then in bank advertisements in small-town newspapers in New York and Iowa in 1927 and 1928. The exact modern wording, "Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity," appears in an advertisement for a loan company in an Ohio newspaper in July 1928.

None of these credit anyone. By 1961 an author introduces it with "as has been so well stated," and a 1970 newspaper calls it an old proverb. That is what it was: an American saying with no known author, passed around because it is good advice that fits on a sign.

## How Seneca got the credit

Names attach to proverbs over time. In 1991 a book of daily readings credited the line to Oprah Winfrey, which shows she was quoted saying it, not that she coined it. The football coach Darrell Royal and the salesman Elmer Letterman are also often named, but the saying is in print before either of their careers, so neither can have originated it.

The earliest book crediting Seneca that the 2010 search found was a 1999 business book, which cites no work of his. In 2008 Bob Woodward's *The War Within* reported General David Petraeus recalling it as "Seneca the Younger's adage," which carried the attribution to a mainstream audience. Quote sites and Stoic accounts on social media did the rest.

So the honest summary is: an American proverb, documented from 1912, given a Roman author around the end of the twentieth century. Nobody has traced the exact moment Seneca's name was added, and we will not pretend to.

## What Seneca actually said about preparation

He did write about the idea, more than once. The line closest in spirit is in his letters:

> If a man knows not to which port he is sailing, no wind is favorable.
>
> — Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 71

It is a harder thought than the proverb. The proverb promises that preparation gets rewarded. Seneca's point is that without a settled aim, nothing that happens to you can count as good fortune, because you would not recognize it.

The other passage people sometimes point to is in *On Benefits*, Book VII, where Seneca quotes the philosopher Demetrius the Cynic:

> "The best wrestler," he would say, "is not he who has learned thoroughly all the tricks and twists of the art, which are seldom met with in actual wrestling, but he who has well and carefully trained himself in one or two of them, and watches keenly for an opportunity of practising them."
>
> — *On Benefits* VII.1, Aubrey Stewart translation (1887)

Some have suggested the proverb is a garbled memory of this. It may be, but no one has shown a chain from one to the other, and the passage says nothing about luck. It is about knowing a few useful principles well enough to use them when the moment comes. Seneca is also quoting someone else, which makes it doubly unsafe to put his name under a paraphrase of it.

## If you want the idea, quote it honestly

The proverb itself is fine. It is useful, and it has a respectable century of American use behind it. Use it as what it is: a saying, author unknown.

If you want Seneca, use Letter 71 and cite it. A quotation with a work and a number attached is one a reader can check, and that is the whole difference between the two.
