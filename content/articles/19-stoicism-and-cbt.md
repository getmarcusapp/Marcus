This is one of the rare cases where a claimed ancient-modern lineage is not a marketing flourish. It is documented, and the people involved said so.

Albert Ellis, who developed Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy in the 1950s, cited Epictetus explicitly as a source and quoted him in his writing. Aaron Beck, who developed cognitive therapy, also traced the philosophical roots of the cognitive model to the Stoics. Neither was reaching for a respectable ancestor after the fact; the idea they were building on is stated plainly in a text they had both read.

## The sentence they were building on

*Enchiridion* 5:

> Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things.

That is the cognitive model in one line, written around AD 125. Events do not cause distress directly. Interpretation intervenes, and interpretation can be examined and revised.

Everything downstream of that in CBT — identifying automatic thoughts, testing them against evidence, noticing cognitive distortions, restructuring — is a clinical method built on a claim the Stoics made first and made central.

## Where the overlap is real

**The mechanism.** Both hold that the judgment is the leverage point, not the event.

**The method.** Stoic *prosochē*, attention to your own impressions as they arrive, is recognizably what a CBT patient is asked to do with a thought record. The [evening examination](/stoic-evening-examination) is structurally a review of the day's automatic thoughts.

**Behavioral practice.** Seneca's instruction to spend days eating plainly and wearing rough clothes, asking "is this what I feared?", is close kin to exposure work: test the catastrophic prediction against reality and watch it shrink.

**The homework.** Both traditions are emphatic that reading about this does nothing. Both assign daily exercises and expect them to be done badly at first.

## Where they genuinely differ

This is the part the enthusiasm usually skips, and it matters.

**The target.** CBT targets distress. It succeeds when symptoms remit. Stoicism targets character, and it is entirely willing to make you more uncomfortable in service of that — the [evening examination](/stoic-evening-examination) is designed to find things you would rather not have found. A Stoic who has become calmer but no more just has not succeeded by Stoic standards.

**The endpoint.** CBT is a treatment with a course and a discharge. Stoicism is a way of life with no completion; the sage is explicitly an ideal nobody reaches.

**The metaphysics.** CBT is deliberately neutral about how the universe is arranged. Stoicism, in its ancient form, rests on a providential rational cosmos, which is a substantive commitment. See [is Stoicism a religion](/is-stoicism-a-religion).

**Values.** CBT does not tell you what to want. Stoicism does: [four virtues](/four-stoic-virtues), and a claim that they are the only genuine good. That is a whole ethics, not a technique.

**The social half.** Justice is a cardinal virtue and *oikeiōsis* extends concern outward to all rational beings. There is no clinical equivalent, and a version of Stoicism that drops it has lost a quarter of the thing.

## The caveat that matters

**Stoicism is not therapy, and it is not a substitute for it.**

The resemblance is close enough that people in genuine difficulty sometimes reach for the philosophy instead of treatment, and that is a bad trade. CBT is a clinical intervention with an evidence base, delivered by trained people, for conditions that do not reliably respond to reading. Stoicism is a philosophy of life for ordinary conditions of living.

They are also not in competition. Plenty of people do both, and the philosophy tends to make more sense to someone who has already done the clinical work, because they have practiced the mechanism.

If you are unwell, get treatment. If you want to know how to live, the Stoics have more to say than any therapist is paid to.

## What each is better at

**CBT is better at** acute distress, specific conditions, structured protocols, and anything requiring a clinician.

**Stoicism is better at** the questions therapy does not address: what to want, what is worth doing, how to treat people, how to hold mortality, how to act when nothing is wrong and you still do not know what your life is for.
