Every night, after the lamp was out and his wife had gone quiet, Seneca put himself on trial.

He describes the habit in *On Anger* III.36, and it is the most detailed account of a daily practice anywhere in Stoic literature. He goes back over the whole day. He examines what he said and what he did. He hides nothing from himself and skips nothing, because, he says, why should he be afraid of any of his mistakes when he can tell himself: see that you do not do it again.

Then he adds the line that makes the practice usable rather than punishing. *I am a judge who does not frighten the defendant.*

That sentence is the entire technique. Everything below is commentary on it.

## Where it comes from

The evening review is older than Stoicism and the Stoics knew it. Epictetus sets it out in *Discourses* III.10 by quoting three questions from the Pythagorean *Golden Verses*:

> Do not allow sleep to close your wearied eyes
> until you have gone over the day three times:
> Where did I go wrong? What did I do? What duty is left undone?

Worth noting, since it is nearly always got wrong online: **Epictetus is quoting.** These are not his lines, and a great deal of the internet attributes them to him without the quotation marks. The Stoics inherited the exercise from the Pythagoreans and made it central.

Seneca is our best witness for what it looked like in practice. Marcus Aurelius is the surviving artefact of someone doing it — the *Meditations* read like the residue of a man conducting this review for twenty years and writing down what he kept having to tell himself.

## The three questions, and what each is for

**Where did I go wrong?**

The scope is narrower than it looks. Not what went badly — that is usually about circumstances, other people, weather, luck. Where did *you* act against what you already knew to be right.

The Stoics are strict about this boundary because everything outside it is outside your control, and reviewing what you could not have changed produces regret rather than improvement. A useful test: if the sentence you write could have been written by a bystander, it belongs to the day's events. If only you could have written it, it belongs in the review.

**What did I do?**

The counterweight, and the question people quietly drop after a week.

Where did you act well? A review that only surfaces failures is not rigorous, it is just gloomy, and it is also inaccurate — you did in fact do some things correctly, and refusing to record them is its own distortion. Name them with the same specificity you bring to the failures. "Was patient" is not an entry. "Did not interrupt when I wanted to" is.

This is the question that keeps the practice survivable. Seneca's judge does not frighten the defendant.

**What duty is left undone?**

The sharpest of the three, and the one with the highest yield.

Failures of commission announce themselves: you snapped at someone, you know it, you feel it. Failures of omission leave no trace at all. The conversation you avoided produced no scene. The work you postponed made no noise. The friend you did not call did not call to complain about it. Nothing happened, so there is nothing to remember, so it never enters an ordinary reflection.

Asking directly is the only way to catch them. It is uncomfortable in a specific way the other two questions are not, which is the sign it is working.

## What it is not

**It is not a diary.** You are not recording what happened. A Stoic review of a day that contained a flight delay, an argument and a good meal might not mention any of the three.

**It is not gratitude journaling.** Gratitude has a place in Stoic practice and the two get blurred constantly. But the evening examination is an audit of your own conduct, not an inventory of what you appreciate. Doing only the second is much pleasanter and does not change how you behave.

**It is not therapy, and it is not not-therapy.** The structural resemblance to cognitive behavioral therapy is real and well documented — CBT's founders acknowledged the Stoic influence directly. But the aim differs. CBT generally targets distress. The Stoic review targets character, and will happily make you briefly more uncomfortable in service of it.

**It is not a performance.** Nobody was ever meant to read the *Meditations*. If you find yourself writing sentences you would be content to have quoted, you have started performing, and the review stops being an instrument.

## How to run it

Ten minutes, at the end of the day, in the same slot every night.

1. **Set the scene the same way each time.** Seneca's trigger was the lamp going out. The consistency of the cue matters more than the quality of the reflection, because the cue is what survives a bad week.
2. **Three questions, in order.** Wrong, then well, then undone. The order is not arbitrary: leading with fault and closing with omission puts the hardest question last, when you are warmed up.
3. **One sentence each is enough.** This is a practice, not an essay. Long entries are usually avoidance wearing a productive costume.
4. **Then set it down.** Add a fourth line naming what you are releasing before sleep. The examination is supposed to close the day. A review that reopens it and hands it to you at 2am has failed at its actual job.
5. **Reread weekly.** Seven entries in a row expose the thing no single entry can: everyone has one recurring failure and one recurring evasion, and both are invisible from inside a single day.

That last step is the one almost nobody does, and it is where most of the compounding happens.

## The two ways it fails

**It becomes flattery.** Every entry finds you basically reasonable, mildly wronged, doing your best. This is comfortable and useless. If a month has passed without the review catching you at something you did not want to write down, it has stopped working.

**It becomes a tribunal.** Every entry is an indictment, the tone hardens, and one evening you decide not to open it. This is the more common failure and it is why Seneca's line about the defendant exists at all. Stoicism holds that you are trying to improve, not that you are contemptible. A practice you abandon in three weeks improves nothing.

The correct register sits between them and it is best described as *interested*. You are examining the conduct of someone whose improvement you are genuinely invested in, without either flattering or prosecuting them.
