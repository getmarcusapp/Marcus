Epictetus wrote nothing.

Everything we have comes through Arrian, a student who took down what was said in the school at Nicopolis and later published it. That single fact explains most of the confusion about which Epictetus book to buy, because the three you will find under his name are not three translations of one work. They are different kinds of object.

## The three

**The *Discourses*** — Arrian's record of Epictetus teaching. Four books survive of an original eight. This is the real thing: an irritable, funny, relentless man arguing with students, losing patience, using examples about wrestling and travel and dinner parties. It is much better than its reputation and much longer than the *Enchiridion*.

**The *Enchiridion*** — the Handbook, also Arrian. Around fifty short chapters distilled from the same teaching into something a person could carry. It opens with the dichotomy of control and never wastes a sentence. This is what most people mean by "Epictetus," and you can read it in under an hour.

**The Art of Living** — Sharon Lebell, 1995. **This one is not a translation.** Its own subtitle calls it a new interpretation, and Lebell rewrites freely: modernizing, compressing, and in places composing sentences that are hers rather than his.

## Which to read first

**Start with the *Enchiridion*.** It is short, it is the concentrate, and if it does not grip you the *Discourses* will not either. Get it in Robin Hard's Oxford World's Classics volume, which contains both the *Discourses* and the *Enchiridion* in one book with good notes. That is the edition we recommend in our [Library](/library).

**Then read the *Discourses* properly.** Most people never do, and it is where Epictetus is actually alive. The handbook gives you the doctrine; the discourses give you the man delivering it, which turns out to matter.

**Read *The Art of Living* if the ancient prose is not landing.** It is a genuinely useful book and an easy door in. Our library recommends it for exactly that: read this, then go to Hard.

## The one rule about Lebell

**Read it. Do not quote from it.**

Because it is an interpretation rather than a translation, its sentences are frequently Lebell's own. On the page, in a book that announces what it is, that is legitimate and often lovely. The problem is what happens when a sentence leaves the book: quote sites strip the subtitle, attach "— Epictetus," and a line written in California in 1995 enters circulation as Roman philosophy.

This is a significant source of fake Epictetus quotes. When we audited our own quote library we found several, including:

> Small-minded people blame others. Average people blame themselves. The wise see all blame as foolishness.

That is a Lebell construction. It is not a translation of anything in the *Enchiridion*, and we removed it. Several others in the same family went with it, which is [why we published the list](/misattributed-stoic-quotes).

None of this is a criticism of Lebell, who was clear about her method. It is a criticism of everyone downstream who dropped the qualifier.

**A practical test:** if you want to quote Epictetus, quote from an edition that gives you a chapter number. *Enchiridion* 8 is checkable. "Epictetus" is not.

## What each one is good for

- **Enchiridion** — memorizing, rereading, carrying. The doctrine at its densest.
- **Discourses** — understanding why any of it works. The arguments, the objections, the temper.
- **The Art of Living** — a first encounter, or a reread when the older prose feels heavy.

For the ideas themselves, the [dichotomy of control](/dichotomy-of-control) is where the *Enchiridion* opens and the best single entry point. If you want the daily practice rather than more reading, start with [how to keep a Stoic journal](/how-to-keep-a-stoic-journal).
