A character named Socrates said it. That is the whole trick, and it is why this one is almost never caught.

"The secret of change is to focus all of your energy not on fighting the old, but on building the new" comes from *Way of the Peaceful Warrior*, Dan Millman's 1980 novel. The narrator, a college gymnast, is taken in hand by an old man working nights at a gas station, and nicknames him Socrates. Soc, for short. He is the one who talks like that.

So somebody quoting "Socrates" from that book is not misreading anything. They are quoting the book accurately. The error happens one step later, when the quotation leaves the novel and the name goes with it.

## A failure mode with no mistake in it

The other entries on [our misattributions list](/misattributed-stoic-quotes) each have a moment where something went wrong. A screenwriter's line got [handed to the emperor it was written about](/did-marcus-aurelius-say-echoes-in-eternity). A summary [lost its author's name in transit](/did-aristotle-say-we-are-what-we-repeatedly-do). A paraphrase [acquired quotation marks](/did-epictetus-say-its-not-what-happens-to-you). A Victorian sentence [was edited until nothing could date it](/who-said-everyone-you-meet-is-fighting-a-hard-battle).

This one has no such moment. Every individual step is defensible. The character is called Socrates. He says the line. Someone writes it down with the name attached, because that is his name. Nobody at any point does anything unreasonable, and a false attribution comes out the other end anyway.

Which makes it the hardest kind to correct. There is no error to point at, only a context that fell away.

## The larger thing this should tell you

Here is the fact that does more work than the correction itself.

**Socrates wrote nothing.** Not a dialogue, not a letter, not a fragment. Not one word survives from his own hand, and there is no evidence he ever wrote any philosophy down. He talked, in public, for free, and then he was executed.

Everything we have comes through other people. Plato, who made him the speaker in dialogues that are increasingly Plato's own philosophy as they go on. Xenophon, who knew him and wrote a plainer, more practical Socrates who sounds notably less like Plato's. Aristophanes, who put him in a comedy as a figure of ridicule while he was still alive. Aristotle, who never met him, reporting at a remove.

Classicists call this the Socratic problem, and it has no solution. There is no method that reliably separates the historical man from the character each author wrote.

So the honest position on any Socrates quotation is that it is, at best, a line given to a character called Socrates by an author with his own purposes. Millman's version is not a break from the tradition. It is the most recent and most literal instance of it.

That is not a defense of the misquote. It is a reason to hold the entire category loosely.

## Two more of his, also on our list

**"Know thyself"** was inscribed at the Temple of Apollo at Delphi and was already an old saying by the time Socrates took it up. He used it. He did not coin it.

**"Beware the barrenness of a busy life"** has no ancient source at all. It reads as twentieth-century English and appears in no Platonic dialogue, which is the only place a saying of his could have come from.

So three of his best-known lines are a novel, a temple wall, and nobody knows.

For contrast, the one that does hold up is "the unexamined life is not worth living," which appears in Plato's *Apology*. That is the account of his trial, and it is the text scholars generally treat as closest to something the historical man actually said. It is not on our list, and it is the Socrates quotation to use if you want one.

## Why this belongs on a Stoic site

Because Socrates is the Stoics' exemplar, and quotes falsely stamped with his name flow straight into Stoic quote culture.

Diogenes Laertius tells the story of Zeno, who was to found the school, reading Xenophon's *Memorabilia* in an Athenian bookshop after a shipwreck, and asking the bookseller where men like that were to be found. The bookseller pointed at Crates the Cynic walking past, and Zeno followed him. On that account Stoicism begins with a book about Socrates.

Epictetus then invokes him constantly, and not as decoration. He is the standard. In the same passage that gave us [another entry on this list](/did-epictetus-say-its-not-what-happens-to-you), the argument that death is not terrible rests on the claim that if it were, it would have looked terrible to Socrates. Marcus and Seneca return to his death repeatedly, as the case study in what the philosophy is actually for.

None of which involves a single sentence Socrates wrote, because there are none. The Stoics did not inherit his aphorisms. They inherited an account of how he lived and how he died, and that is the part that was never quotable in the first place.

## What to read instead

Two short texts, and between them you get both Socrates.

Plato's *Apology* is the trial speech: forty pages, no technical philosophy, and the one place the historical voice comes through with least interference. Xenophon's *Memorabilia* is the other portrait, more domestic and less literary, and it is the book that reportedly started Stoicism.

Read them and the misquote loses its appeal on its own, without needing to be corrected. The real thing is stranger and more specific than any aphorism anyone has since put in his mouth.
