The Stoics

Twelve figures, in the order they lived. A shipwrecked merchant who founded a school on a public porch, a slave who taught emperors, and the most powerful man alive writing privately to keep himself honest. Who each one was, what they taught, and the book worth reading first.

The school

Zeno of Citium

c. 334–262 BC

A shipwrecked merchant who founded a school on a public porch.

Cleanthes

Cleanthes

c. 330–230 BC

A boxer who carried water by night so he could study by day.

Chrysippus

c. 279–206 BC

The logician who turned a set of convictions into a system.

Cato the Younger

95–46 BC

Wrote nothing. Held up by every Stoic after him as the thing itself.

Seneca

c. 4 BC–65 AD

Nero's tutor, enormously rich, and the most quotable Stoic on how to live poor.

Musonius Rufus

Musonius Rufus

c. 30–101 AD

Epictetus's teacher, and the most practical of the Roman Stoics.

Epictetus

c. 50–135 AD

Born a slave, lame, and the most uncompromising teacher in the tradition.

Marcus Aurelius

121–180 AD

The most powerful man alive, writing privately to keep himself honest.

Sources & influences

Heraclitus

c. 535–475 BC

Supplied the Stoics with their physics, two centuries before there were any.

Diogenes of Sinope

c. 412–323 BC

The Cynic whose students taught Zeno. Stoicism starts here, in a barrel.

Cicero

106–43 BC

Not a Stoic. The reason Stoic ethics survived into the Latin West.

Diogenes Laertius

Diogenes Laertius

c. 3rd century AD

An uncritical compiler, and the reason we know the early Stoics at all.
