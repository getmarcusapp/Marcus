The Dichotomy of Control

By Gio White·How sources are checked

Epictetus opens his handbook with it, and everything else in Stoicism follows from it:

Some things are up to us and some are not. Up to us are opinion, impulse, desire, aversion, and in a word whatever is our own doing. Not up to us are the body, property, reputation, office, and in a word whatever is not our own doing.Enchiridion 1

This is the dichotomy of control, and it is the most quoted idea in Stoicism, the most immediately useful, and the most consistently flattened in the retelling.

The flattened version is control what you can control. That is not wrong exactly, but it has been absorbed into sports psychology and productivity advice in a way that loses the part that does the work. Epictetus is not offering a focusing technique. He is making a claim about what is actually yours.

The list is shorter than people think

Notice what is on the "up to us" side. Not your actions. Not your effort. Not your work.

Opinion, impulse, desire, aversion — and Epictetus’s summary term for the whole faculty is prohairesis, usually rendered as choice, will, or the faculty of moral judgment. What is genuinely yours is the assenting: the moment where an impression arrives and you either accept it, act on it, or let it pass.

Everything downstream of that is contaminated by the world. You can decide to make the phone call; whether your voice works, whether the line connects, whether they answer, whether they listen, is not up to you. Even your effort is not fully yours, since it depends on a body that gets ill and a brain that gets tired.

This is why the dichotomy is more radical than the poster version. The Stoics are not saying focus on your effort and let go of results. They are saying the only thing wholly in your possession is the judgment you make in this moment, and that everything else — health, reputation, wealth, your own body, the people you love — is on loan.

And your thoughts are not on the "up to us" side either. This surprises people. Thoughts arrive unbidden; you did not choose the intrusive one. What is up to you is whether you assent to it, dwell on it, act on it. Stoic practice is not thought suppression. It is discernment: this impression is mine to engage with, that one I can let pass.

Why it works

Epictetus’s argument is not that accepting limits feels nicer. It is causal.

Remember that what is insulting is not the person who abuses you, but the judgment about them that they are insulting.Enchiridion 20

If distress lives in the judgment rather than the event, and judgment is the one thing you own, then the entire mechanism of your own suffering sits inside the only territory you control. That is either the most demanding claim in ancient philosophy or the most liberating one, depending on the day.

The practical consequence is that most anxiety turns out to be misallocated attention: effort spent on a region you have no authority over, at the cost of the region where you have total authority.

The honest complication

Modern Stoics have argued the dichotomy is too clean, and the objection deserves an answer rather than a hand-wave.

William Irvine proposed a trichotomy in A Guide to the Good Life: things we fully control, things we control not at all, and a large middle category we partially influence — which is where most of life sits. His fix is to internalize the goal: not "win the match" but "play as well as I can."

Classicists generally push back, arguing the trichotomy misreads Epictetus rather than improving him, since the original already handles the middle by locating your goal in the choosing rather than the achieving. Both positions are defensible.

The practical upshot is the same either way, and it is worth stating plainly: the dichotomy is a tool for locating your goal, not a claim that outcomes do not matter. A Stoic doctor still tries to save the patient. What changes is where the doctor’s peace of mind is anchored.

The misuse worth naming

The dichotomy becomes a problem when it is used as permission.

Other people’s behavior is not in my control is true, and it is also available as a justification for tolerating what you should be addressing, or for declining responsibility for your effect on others. Epictetus does not draw that conclusion, and neither does any Stoic: justice is one of the four cardinal virtues, and acting rightly toward others is squarely inside the territory that is yours.

Similarly, "it is not up to me" is not a license for passivity about injustice. Marcus Aurelius ran an empire. Cato died opposing one. The dichotomy told them where their equanimity came from, not whether to act.

If the idea is consistently making you more comfortable and less engaged, it has been misapplied.

How to practice it daily

The dichotomy is not a belief to hold. It is a sorting operation you run, and it works best written down.

Each morning, take the day as it is actually scheduled and split it:

  1. Name what is genuinely at stake today. The meeting, the diagnosis, the conversation, the deadline.
  2. For each, write what is yours. Almost always some version of: how I prepare, what I say, how I treat the people involved, whether I tell the truth.
  3. Write what is not. Their reaction. The decision. Whether it is fair. How it lands.
  4. Release the second list explicitly. Not as a feeling but as a sentence. Naming it is what stops it running in the background all day.

Two minutes. The value is not insight — you knew most of it already — but the act of separating, which does not happen on its own.

Then in the evening, check: where did I spend today? Most people discover they spent it almost entirely on the second list.

Further reading
