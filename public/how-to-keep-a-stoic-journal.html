The practice

How to Keep a Stoic Journal

By Gio White·How sources are checked

The most famous book in Stoicism is a journal. Marcus Aurelius never gave it a title, never showed it to anyone, and would probably have been appalled to learn it was published. The Greek heading it carries in the manuscripts is Ta eis heauton: to himself. What we call the Meditations is the private notebook of a man talking himself back into shape, night after night, in army camps on the Danube.

That is the model. Not a diary of events, not a gratitude list, and not a record of how the day made you feel. A Stoic journal is a working instrument for changing how you meet the next one.

Here is the practice as the Stoics actually described it, and how to run it daily without it becoming another thing you have abandoned by February.

What makes a journal Stoic

Three features separate this from ordinary journaling, and all three come from the sources.

It is structured by questions, not by prompts about feeling. Seneca describes his own nightly practice in On Anger III.36: when the lamp is out and the house has gone quiet, he says, he examines the whole of his day and goes back over what he has done and said, hiding nothing from himself and excusing nothing. He calls himself before his own court. The point is not to record the day. It is to audit it.

It is adversarial toward yourself, and gentle about it. Seneca’s line about the practice is that he is a judge who does not frighten the defendant. That balance is the hard part and the whole art. A journal that never catches you doing wrong is flattery. A journal that only catches you is a machine for producing shame, and you will stop opening it. Both failures end the practice.

It is repetitive on purpose. Read the Meditations straight through and the striking thing is how often Marcus tells himself the same three or four things. People will be difficult today. You will die. The only thing that is actually yours is how you respond. He is not developing an argument. He is doing reps. If your journal entries start to look repetitive after a month, that is not a sign the practice has gone stale. That is what practice looks like from the inside.

The evening examination

This is the core, and it is the oldest documented form. Start here if you start anywhere.

Epictetus gives it in Discourses III.10, quoting three questions from the Pythagorean Golden Verses — worth noting that he is quoting, not coining, since half the internet attributes these to Epictetus himself:

Where did I go wrong?What did I do?What duty is left undone?

Three questions, in that order, about the day now behind you. Ten minutes. In practice each one does distinct work:

Where did I go wrong? Not what went badly, which is usually about circumstances. Where did you act against what you know to be right. The scope is deliberately narrow: your judgments, your choices, your words.

What did I do? The counterweight, and the one people skip. Where did you act well? Stoicism is not self-flagellation, and a review that only finds faults is not accurate, it is just pessimistic. Name what you got right with the same specificity you name what you did not.

What duty is left undone? The sharpest of the three. Not what you did badly but what you did not do at all: the conversation avoided, the work postponed, the person not called. Omissions are invisible to most self-reflection because nothing happened, and nothing happening leaves no trace to notice.

Add a fourth if you want the practice to help you sleep rather than keep you awake: what am I carrying that I can set down? The examination is meant to close the day, not reopen it.

The morning preparation

Less documented than the evening review, but it follows directly from Stoic doctrine and Marcus clearly did some version of it — Book II opens with him telling himself, at dawn, exactly who he is going to have to deal with.

The morning has a different job. The evening judges what happened; the morning arranges how you will meet what has not happened yet. Two questions carry most of the weight:

What is in my control today, and what must I release? This is the dichotomy of control applied to a specific day rather than held as an abstraction. Epictetus opens the Enchiridion with it: some things are up to us, some are not. Up to us are our judgments and intentions. Not up to us are outcomes, other people, reputation, weather. Writing the day’s version of that list takes two minutes and defuses a surprising amount of what would otherwise become anxiety by lunchtime.

What difficulty might I face today, and how would a person of good character meet it? This is premeditatio malorum, premeditation of adversity, and it is the Stoic exercise modern research has been most enthusiastic about rediscovering. Not catastrophizing. Naming one specific plausible difficulty and rehearsing your response, so that if it arrives you are executing a plan rather than improvising while upset.

How to actually keep it up

The failure mode of Stoic journaling is not writing badly. It is stopping.

Ten minutes, not thirty. Seneca’s review happens after the lamp goes out, at the end of the day, when he is tired. It is short by design. A practice you can do badly on a bad day survives; a practice that requires a good day does not.

Same time, attached to something you already do. Marcus wrote on campaign, which is to say under worse conditions than yours. The consistency came from the slot, not the mood.

Write in the second person if the first feels strange. Marcus does this constantly: you have power over your mind, remember how long you have been putting this off. It sounds odd until you try it, and then it turns out that addressing yourself is what makes the thing an instrument rather than a record.

Never break the chain twice. Missing one day is nothing. Missing two is how practices end. If you want a rule, make it that one.

Expect the first two weeks to feel like nothing. The value is cumulative and it arrives sideways: you catch yourself mid-reaction one afternoon, weeks in, and recognize the thing you wrote about on Tuesday. That is the return, and it does not come in the first entry.

A week of it

If you want a structure to start with rather than a blank page:

  • Morning, two minutes. What is mine today, what is not. Where courage will be required.
  • Evening, eight minutes. The three questions. Then one thing you are setting down.
  • Once a week, fifteen minutes. Read back the seven entries and look for the repeat. Everyone has one recurring failure and one recurring evasion, and neither is visible from inside a single day. The week is the unit where patterns become legible.

That last one is the step almost nobody does, and it is where most of the value is.

Where to read further

The three primary texts, in the translations worth owning, are on our Library page, and the people behind them are on The Stoics. If you want the short version: Marcus Aurelius for the journal itself, Epictetus for the method, Seneca for the company.

One caution, and it is the reason we wrote a whole page about it: a great deal of what circulates as Stoic wisdom is not. If a line you want to build a practice on has a name attached but no book and chapter, check it before you build on it.
