On weight, not length

"Let us see to it, my dear Lucilius, that our lives, like jewels of great price, be noteworthy not because of their width but because of their weight. Let us measure them by their performance, not by their duration." — Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 93

Seneca wrote this letter to answer a specific grief. Lucilius had sent word mourning the philosopher Metronax, and the mourning carried a complaint underneath it: the man had died too soon, he should have been granted more years. Seneca refuses the premise. He will not let a life be totaled up in seasons survived. A life is not wide, he argues, it is heavy or it is light, and the counting of birthdays tells you nothing about which. The letter is his correction of a friend who mistook duration for sufficiency.

This week in Venice, George Clooney accepted the festival's Golden Lion for lifetime achievement, and he poked fun at it, saying there are really only two things that come to mind: what an incredible honor this is, and then, "do they know something that I don't?" The joke names a real unease. A lifetime award treats a life as finished, tallied, closed. Seneca's answer is that no ceremony can measure the thing anyway. The weight was there or it wasn't, long before the applause.

If your life were weighed today rather than counted, what would it be found to hold?
