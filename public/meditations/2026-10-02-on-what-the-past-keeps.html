On what the past keeps

"The past is the one period of our life which is sacred and set apart, which has passed beyond all human risks and is removed from Fortune's sway." — Seneca, On the Shortness of Life, 10

Seneca wrote this essay to his father-in-law Paulinus, who held a senior post managing Rome's grain supply, and the argument it answers is a practical one: Paulinus had spent his life absorbed in a demanding office, and Seneca urges him to withdraw before the work consumes what remains of him. To make the case, Seneca divides life into three parts: the present, which slips away as we hold it; the future, which is uncertain; and the past, which alone cannot be taken back once it is lived. The busy man, he argues, never looks back, because a life spent only reacting leaves nothing worth returning to. The past is secure only for those who built something in it.

This week the African American Civil War Museum in Washington reopened in its permanent home after years of renovation and fundraising. It tells the story of the United States Colored Troops who fought for the Union cause in the Civil War after President Abraham Lincoln called for their enlistment. The 16,000 square foot museum reopened with admission free, and plans a Veterans Day reading of the names of the soldiers inscribed on the Spirit of Freedom Memorial. What is kept and named cannot be undone by time. That is the one possession fortune cannot reach.

What are you building now that you will be glad to turn back and find, years from here, beyond the reach of chance?
