On the temporary exhibit

"All things are passing away, and we ourselves are being carried past in the very stream." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VI.15

The line sits inside a longer argument Marcus makes against clinging to any single thing as though it could be held. He is not mourning. He is correcting a habit of mind: the tendency to treat what is in front of us as fixed, when its whole nature is to move on. The image he reaches for is a river you are standing in, not watching from the bank. You are not spared the current; you are inside it.

Yesterday the main floors of the Whitney Biennial in New York came down, closing the 82nd edition of an exhibition that had been assembled since March. The 2026 Whitney Biennial, the 82nd edition of the Whitney Museum's art biennial, ran from March 8th to August 23rd, 2026. A biennial is built to end. The work was gathered, arranged, argued over by critics, walked through by strangers, and then, on a scheduled date, taken apart. Most of what we build has that shape, though we rarely see the closing date printed on the wall. A show that announces its own last day is only being honest about a condition everything shares.

If you knew the exact date the thing you are working on would be taken down, would you tend it more carefully or hold it more loosely?
