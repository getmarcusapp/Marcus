On the careful householder

"But we should play the part of a careful householder; we should increase what we have inherited." — Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 64

Seneca wrote Letter 64 the morning after a dinner where the talk turned to the great dead, philosophers whose books the guests had read aloud. He is answering a small ingratitude he sees in his age: the habit of treating past thinkers as finished monuments to admire from a distance. No, he insists. Their work was not a museum piece but a bequest. They toiled and stored up a treasure so those who came later could take it up and add to it. The duty of the heir is not reverence alone. It is stewardship: to receive the thing intact and hand it on enlarged.

This week a film society in Salt Lake City set a target for reopening the Tower Theatre, a single-screen cinema that opened in 1928 and has sat shuttered for years. They are aiming for 2028, the building's hundredth year, and still lack much of the money. Nobody restoring a near-century-old room does it for themselves alone. They are doing what Seneca describes: keeping a treasure whole and paying it forward, so audiences of an age they will not see can gasp at a film in the dark.

What did you inherit that you are meant not merely to keep, but to hand on larger than you received it?
