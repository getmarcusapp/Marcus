On exile

"If you are that good man and have his virtues, exile will not harm or degrade you, because the virtues are present in you which are most able to help and to sustain you." — Musonius Rufus, Lectures, IX (That Exile Is Not an Evil)

Musonius delivered this lecture as consolation to a man newly banished, and he was not speaking from theory. Nero exiled him to a barren island; a later emperor banished him again. His argument here is deliberately hard: a person carries his character wherever the state sends him, and a sentence of removal touches the place, not the soul. What the exile keeps is the one thing no decree can revoke.

Seven hundred and five years ago today, on 14 September 1321, Dante Alighieri died in exile in Ravenna. He had been driven from Florence in 1302 and never allowed home. It was during that exile that he conceived and wrote the Divine Comedy, the work that made his banishment irrelevant to his standing. Most of us will not be exiled. But we all know the fear of being cut off: from a job, a city, a circle that defined us. Musonius insists the question is not where you are sent but what you bring.

If you lost the place and the people that currently tell you who you are, what part of yourself would remain untouched?
