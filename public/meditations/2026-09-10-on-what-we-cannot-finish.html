On what we cannot finish

"Never say about anything, 'I have lost it,' but only 'I have given it back.'" — Epictetus, Enchiridion, 11

The eleventh chapter of the Enchiridion is not written to comfort a grieving man in the abstract. Epictetus is coaching a specific reflex out of his students: the instinct to speak of a lost child, a lost farm, a lost position as if it had been torn away rather than merely recalled by whoever lent it. His argument is grammatical before it is philosophical. Change the verb, he says, and you change what the loss can do to you. What you built, planned, or held was always on loan, and the terms were never yours to set.

This week Hungary's new government cancelled the New National Gallery in Budapest. The SANAA-designed museum, meant to be the centrepiece of the Liget City Park project, has been cancelled, and a decree brought all construction work to an end, with the remaining building sites to be reinstated as public parkland. A design its own director general called world-class exists complete on paper and will not stand where it was meant to. Anyone who has poured years into something later shelved knows the ache. The work was real. The claim on its arrival never was.

What are you currently holding as if you owned it, when the honest word is that it was only ever lent to you?
