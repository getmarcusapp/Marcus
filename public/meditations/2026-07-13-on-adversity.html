On adversity

The greater the difficulty, the more glory in surmounting it. Skillful pilots gain their reputation from storms and tempests. — Epictetus, as recorded in the Discourses

Epictetus taught that hardship is not the opposite of a good life but the raw material of one. He used the image of an athlete: no one becomes formidable by training against weak opponents. The difficulty is not in the way of the work. The difficulty is the work.

This is one of the most misread Stoic ideas. It is not a call to seek out suffering, nor a claim that everything happens for a reason. It is narrower and more useful than that: when difficulty arrives, as it will, it is also the only occasion on which certain virtues can be practiced at all. You cannot practice patience in the absence of provocation, or courage in the absence of fear. The storm is not a detour from becoming a skillful pilot. It is the test that makes the skill real.

What difficulty in front of you right now is also an opportunity to practice something you claim to value?
