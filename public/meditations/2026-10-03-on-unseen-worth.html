On unseen worth

"Can one by scanting praise depreciate gold, ivory, or purple, a lyre or a dagger, a flower or a shrub?" — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.20

The line is the close of an argument Marcus is having with himself about applause. He has just insisted that anything truly beautiful owes nothing to being noticed, that praise adds no grace and blame subtracts none. He is not consoling a reader. He is trying to break his own habit of setting his hopes, as he puts it, on the report of others. The emperor who could command any audience in the world is reminding himself that the opinion of that audience changes nothing about the thing itself.

This week a small painting proved his point in dollars. A long-lost work by the Chicago artist Gertrude Abercrombie, bought for thirty dollars at a Michigan estate sale, sold at auction for $1.35 million, a record for her. The 8-by-11-inch painting, titled Alderman Merriam's Shells and made in 1951, topped $1 million on Thursday. The picture was exactly as good at thirty dollars as it was at a million. Only our seeing of it moved. Worth sat quietly in a drawer for decades, unchanged, waiting for the right eyes.

What of real value are you holding right now that you discount only because no one around you has priced it yet?
