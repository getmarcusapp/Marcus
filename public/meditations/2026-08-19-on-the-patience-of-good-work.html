On the patience of good work

"No great thing is created suddenly, any more than a bunch of grapes or a fig. If you tell me that you desire a fig, I answer that there must be time. Let it first blossom, then bear fruit, then ripen." — Epictetus, Discourses I.15

The line answers a student who wanted virtue at once, the way a man might want dinner served. Epictetus refuses the shortcut. He reaches for the plainest thing in the yard, a fig, to make an argument about time: the fruit you crave cannot be hurried into being by wanting it, and neither can a steady character. His whole point is that the desire is not the achievement, and that anyone impatient with the ripening has misunderstood what he is asking for.

This week the Australian National University reopened University House in Canberra, its ceremonial hall shuttered since a hailstorm shattered the roof in January 2020. The Commonwealth heritage-listed building was severely damaged in the January 2020 Canberra hailstorm and was officially reopened more than six years later. Six years for a roof and its rooms. We tend to measure such delays as loss. The Stoic measures them as the necessary weather of anything worth having back: blossom, fruit, then ripeness, in that order and no faster.

What are you rushing right now that would be better for being left to ripen?
