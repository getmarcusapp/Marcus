On the examined day

I will keep constant watch over myself and, most usefully, will put each day up for review. — Seneca, Letters to Lucilius I

Seneca wrote the Letters in the last years of his life, after Nero, his former student, had exiled him and stripped him of most of his wealth. He was in his late sixties, writing to his friend Lucilius in a sustained correspondence that reads less like advice and more like thinking out loud. The daily review he describes here wasn't a productivity ritual. It was an accounting.

The Stoics distinguished between examining and judging. The evening review wasn't about guilt, it was about clarity. What did I actually do today? Where did I act from fear rather than from reason? What would I do differently? Three questions, asked honestly, before sleep. Seneca believed that a life not regularly examined had no real direction, only momentum.

What happened today that you haven't fully looked at yet?
