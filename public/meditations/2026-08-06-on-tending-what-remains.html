On tending what remains

"That which has grown old is the very thing which time changes: nothing perishes wholly." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations VIII.6

Marcus wrote this against a fear he names openly elsewhere in the same book: the dread that a thing, once gone, is gone for good. His answer is not consolation but physics as he understood it. Nothing is destroyed; it is only reassigned. The plank rots, but the substance of the plank returns to the world and becomes something else. What we call ruin is a building's material waiting to be taken up again by careful hands. The Stoic sees decay and continuity as the same motion viewed from two sides.

This week a coastal California building that opened in 1915 as the town's first hospital reopened as a small inn, restored by a woman whose grandmother once nursed there. Natalie Dingman, a native of Fort Bragg, said the building has always been part of her family's story, as her grandmother worked there as a nurse: coming home to restore it felt deeply personal. A structure does not survive by staying the same. It survives by being taken up again, given a new use, and kept in the world by someone willing to do the work.

What around you is not dying but waiting to be tended into its next form?
