On the two handles

Everything has two handles: the one by which it may be borne, the other by which it may not. If your brother acts unjustly, do not lay hold on the act by that handle wherein he acts unjustly, for this is the handle which cannot be borne; but lay hold on the other, that he is your brother, that he was nurtured with you, and you will lay hold on the thing by that handle by which it can be borne. — Epictetus, Enchiridion 43

Epictetus taught at Nicopolis, in what is now western Greece, after Domitian expelled the philosophers from Rome in the 90s AD. The Enchiridion is not his book: it is a handbook his student Arrian assembled from lecture notes, which is why it reads like a set of instructions rather than an argument.

This one is a practical instruction about attention. The same event genuinely has more than one true description, and the description you reach for determines whether you can carry it. He is not asking you to pretend the injustice did not happen. The unjust act is real, and it is the handle that cannot be borne. What he is pointing out is that it is not the only handle, and that which one you take hold of is a choice you are making whether or not you notice making it.

What are you holding by the handle that cannot be borne?
