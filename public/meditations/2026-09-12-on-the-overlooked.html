On the overlooked

"We must also observe closely points of this kind, that even the secondary effects of Nature's processes possess a sort of grace and attraction." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book 3.2

The passage answers a habit of the eye, not a crisis of state. Marcus is teaching himself to look at things he has already stopped seeing. He turns to the cracked crust of a baking loaf, the split skin of a ripe fig, the furrowed brow of an old man, and insists that even these unintended by-products carry a kind of grace. The point is discipline of attention: the beauty is not added to the world but discovered in it, by a person willing to slow down long enough to notice what everyone else walks past.

This week London begins its Open House festival, when hundreds of buildings across the city that are normally closed unlock their doors to the public for free, from 12 to 20 September, spanning all 33 boroughs. The premise is quietly radical: the extraordinary is often the ordinary, seen at last. We move through streets whose doorways we never enter, past facades we have filed under "familiar" and therefore invisible. The festival does not build anything new. It only invites people to look at what was always there.

What do you pass every day without seeing, and what would change if today you actually looked?
