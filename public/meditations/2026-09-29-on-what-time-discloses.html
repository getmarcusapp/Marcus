On what time discloses

We should always allow some time to elapse, for time discloses the truth. — Seneca, On Anger, Book II

The line sits inside a practical argument, not a reflection on memory. In On Anger, Seneca is building a case against acting on first impressions of injury. His counsel is procedural: when you feel wronged, suspend the verdict, because anger convicts before the evidence is in and the passage of hours often clears what looked like malice. Delay, for Seneca, is not weakness. It is the only condition under which the truth of a thing can surface. He addressed the essay to his brother Novatus, a man of temper, and the whole book reads as an attempt to slow a hot mind down long enough to see straight.

This week a Florida mayor found a religious icon that had left Tarpon Springs in 1923, sent to Greece for repair after a fire and never returned. Damage from a 1923 fire forced the painting to be sent back to its original artist in Aegina for restoration, and it was never returned to Florida, leaving the cathedral without the piece for 103 years, until it was found hanging on the back wall of a small parish church. A century did what no search could: it kept the thing safe and then, unhurried, gave it back.

What in your life have you already judged, that another few days might show you differently?
