On carrying meaning

"No man was ever wise by chance." — Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 76

Seneca wrote Letter 76 to defend a strange habit: at an advanced age, and famous, he had gone back to sit among students at a philosopher's lectures. Friends mocked him for it. His answer was that wisdom is not a windfall you stumble into but a thing built by deliberate, repeated, unglamorous labor. You return to the bench because the work is never finished and never automatic. As long as you live, you must keep learning how to live. The line about wisdom by chance is his refusal of shortcuts: nothing worth understanding arrives without being earned.

This week the classicist Emily Wilson published a new book about exactly this kind of labor. Fresh off publishing her groundbreaking translations of The Iliad and The Odyssey, Wilson explores perceptions and misperceptions of the ancient world in Crossing the Wine-Dark Sea. A translator does not receive meaning intact. She carries it across a gap, losing something at every step, choosing each word against a hundred rejected ones. It is slow, deliberate, unfinished work: the opposite of chance. What she demonstrates is what Seneca claimed. Understanding is not given. It is made, line by line, by someone willing to keep doing it.

What understanding in your life have you been waiting to arrive on its own, when it will only ever come from returning to the work?
