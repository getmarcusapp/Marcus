On what time marks

"We should remember that even Nature's inadvertence has its own charm, its own attractiveness." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, 3.2

This passage sits inside an argument Marcus is having with his own aversion. He has just been cataloguing decay: the cracked crust of a baking loaf, the split skin of a ripe fig, the drooping heads of grain, the foam on a boar's mouth. These are the byproducts of things ripening and breaking down, and the ordinary response is to look away. Marcus stops himself. He insists that a person who has looked closely at how the world actually works will find grace precisely in the flaw, the fissure, the sign of age. The point is not to prettify decline but to correct the reflex that treats every mark of time as a defect to be hidden.

This week a restored version of the 1994 film 1942: A Love Story returns to Indian cinemas, rebuilt in 8K so a new audience can see it as its makers first intended. Restoration is a strange act of respect. It does not pretend the decades never passed; it works with what age left behind and hands the thing forward. Most of what we inherit reaches us cracked in some way. The question is whether we can see the cracks and still want to keep it.

What worn or aging thing in your life have you been treating as ruined, when it is only marked?
