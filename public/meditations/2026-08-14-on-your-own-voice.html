On your own voice

"Let there be a difference between yourself and your book!" — Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 33

Lucilius had asked Seneca to keep signing off his letters the way he had before: with a tidy borrowed maxim from one of the Stoic founders. Seneca refuses. The whole letter is an argument against collecting other people's best lines and mistaking them for wisdom of your own. Knowing something, he insists, means making it yours, not glancing back at the master for permission. Anyone who only repeats what Zeno or Cleanthes said is a reporter, not a thinker. At some point the student has to become a teacher and speak in a voice that is his.

This week the Nigerian singer Ayra Starr releases her third album, Starr Girl, built partly on international collaborations. She has to prove that international collaborations can expand her audience without making her own artistic voice secondary to the names attached to the album. That is Seneca's problem exactly, moved into a recording studio: how to absorb everything around you without dissolving into it. Influence is not the danger. Disappearing into your influences is.

Which of the opinions you hold most firmly have you actually tested, and which are you simply repeating because someone you admired said them well?
