On endurance

"Fire tests gold, adversity tests strong men." — Seneca, On Providence

Seneca wrote On Providence to answer a hard question: why do good people suffer? He was writing under Nero, in a court where a wrong word could mean exile or death. He had already survived one banishment. He knew adversity not as a topic but as a condition of his days. His answer was not that suffering is good, but that it reveals what a person is made of, the way fire reveals whether metal is gold.

This week the 40th-anniversary concert tour of Les Misérables reached its final stop in New York, closing a run that spanned seventeen countries and audiences in the thousands. Its producer named the reason the show still fills halls: the survival of the human spirit. Victor Hugo built that story from want, imprisonment, and grief, and audiences return to it because they recognize the terrain. Seneca and Hugo agree on something plain: what endures in a person is not visible until it is tested.

What in you has only ever been proven by the things you would not have chosen?
