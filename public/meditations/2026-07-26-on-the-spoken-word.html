On the spoken word

"If you didn't learn these things in order to demonstrate them in practice, what did you learn them for?" — Epictetus, Discourses

Epictetus wrote nothing. Born a slave in Phrygia and later lame from his bondage, he taught philosophy aloud in a small school in Nicopolis, insisting that ideas mattered only when they changed how a person lived and spoke. Everything we have of him survives because a student, Arrian, sat and transcribed the lectures. The words we read were first breath in a room: a voice, an audience, a moment that could not be repeated. Philosophy, for him, was performance in the truest sense: something enacted, not filed away.

This week the poet Black Ice died at 54. Born Lamar Manson, he was widely recognized as the first poet signed to Def Jam Records and as a star of the Def Poetry Jam TV and Broadway shows, a Tony-, Peabody-, and Emmy Award-winning artist. He built a body of work that lived in the throat, not the archive. It is worth remembering that the oldest way to carry a thought is still the most fragile: one person speaking, another listening closely enough to keep it.

What have you learned that you have never once said aloud to another person?
