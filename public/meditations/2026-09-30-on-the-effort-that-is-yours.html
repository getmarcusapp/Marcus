On the effort that is yours

"For it is only what belongs to himself that he makes the matter for his activity." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 10.33

Book Ten of the Meditations circles back, again and again, to a single distinction Marcus keeps trying to hold steady in his own mind: the difference between the exertion a person owns and the result the world hands back. This passage sits inside a longer argument against the excuse that circumstances make virtue impossible. Marcus answers himself. A man's material is only what falls to him: his own choices, his own labor, the portion assigned. Everything downstream of that, whether the effort lands or fails, belongs to the sum total of things, not to him. He is not consoling a reader. He is refusing himself the exit of blaming the outcome.

On Sunday in Berlin, Tigst Assefa ran inside world-record pace for nearly the whole marathon, then slowed sharply in the final two kilometers as her body failed. She won in 2 hours, 11 minutes, 4 seconds, the third-fastest women's marathon ever, but sustained an Achilles tear. The record she wanted stayed out of reach. What she actually controlled, the running, she gave completely. The rest was never hers to hand herself.

If the result of your best effort today is taken out of your hands, will you have done the part that was yours to do?
