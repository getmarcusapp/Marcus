“A Gem Cannot Be Polished Without Friction”: Not Seneca or Confucius

By Gio White·How sources are checked

When a saying is credited to Seneca on one website and to Confucius on the next, that is usually the first sign it belongs to neither. Two traditions a continent apart rarely produce the same sentence.

"A gem cannot be polished without friction, nor a man perfected without trials" is that kind of saying. Quote sites give it to Seneca. Others give it to Confucius. None of them gives a book, a chapter, or a letter number, because there isn’t one.

Where we found it

The fact-checking sites that usually settle these, Quote Investigator, Barry Popik’s research, and Wikiquote’s misattribution lists, have nothing on this one. So we looked.

The earliest printing we could find is a chapter title in a Victorian adventure novel for boys: William Dalton’s The Wolf-Boy of China. The story is set in China, and every chapter is headed by a proverb, things like "Every day cannot be a feast of lanterns." Chapter II is headed:

THE GEM CANNOT BE POLISHED WITHOUT FRICTION, NOR MAN PERFECTED WITHOUT TRIALS.William Dalton, The Wolf-Boy of China, chapter II

So by the nineteenth century, an English writer was using it as a proverb in a book about China, with no author attached. Where Dalton found it, we don’t know. It may come from one of the English collections of Chinese sayings published earlier in the century; it may be Dalton’s own rendering. And this is only the earliest version we have found. Someone may find an older one, and if you do, we would like to hear about it.

What we can say is that the earliest version we found is an anonymous proverb, not Confucius, and certainly not Seneca.

The Chinese line it resembles

There is a famous classical Chinese saying with a similar image, from the Book of Rites, in a chapter on education. In James Legge’s 1885 translation:

The jade uncut will not form a vessel for use; and if men do not learn, they do not know the way (in which they should go).Li Ki (Book of Rites), "Hsio Kî," trans. James Legge (1885)

It is tempting to call this the original. We won’t, because nothing connects it to Dalton’s wording, and it says something different: it is about learning, not hardship. The polished jade is an educated person, not a suffering one.

What Seneca actually said

Seneca never wrote the gem line, but it is easy to see why it drifted to him. He wrote a whole essay, On Providence, on why good people suffer, and his answer is that hardship is how character gets tested and proved. In Aubrey Stewart’s 1889 translation:

Fire tries gold, misfortune tries brave men.Seneca, On Providence V, trans. Aubrey Stewart

And, shorter still:

misfortune is virtue’s opportunity.Seneca, On Providence IV, trans. Aubrey Stewart

That is the real Stoic version of the idea, and it is harder than the proverb. The gem line is consoling: trials will polish you. Seneca’s point is that they will test you, and that passing the test is up to you. Gold that is tried by fire can also be found to be something else.

What to quote

If you want the proverb, call it a proverb, author unknown. That is the honest credit. If you want Seneca, quote On Providence.

This is on our list of misattributed Stoic quotes, along with another Seneca line that turned out to be an American saying from 1912.

Sources: William Dalton, The Wolf-Boy of China, chapter II (scan of an American edition on archive.org); Li Ki, trans. James Legge, Sacred Books of the East vol. 28 (1885), on archive.org; Seneca, On Providence, trans. Aubrey Stewart, Minor Dialogues (1889), Project Gutenberg #64576.
