What the Stoics Said About Anger

Seneca wrote three books about anger, which tells you how seriously the Stoics took it. He begins by calling it the most hideous and frenzied of all the emotions, and by describing what it does to a face.

His central claim is unfashionable and he knew it: anger is never useful.

This was a live argument. Aristotle held that anger, in the right amount and toward the right person, is appropriate and even necessary — that a man who does not get angry at genuine injustice is deficient. Seneca takes this on directly and rejects it. You do not need anger to fight, he says, or to punish, or to correct. Reason does all of it better, and unlike anger, reason stops when the job is done.

Anger as temporary madness

Seneca’s definition is that anger is a brief insanity: it cannot be controlled once admitted, it does not listen, it wants punishment more than it wants the right outcome, and it damages the person holding it more reliably than the person it is aimed at.

The mechanism matters, because it is where the practical work lives. Anger is not a reflex. It has a structure:

  1. The first movement. Something happens and you feel the jolt. Involuntary, physical, not yet anger. Seneca is explicit that the wise person has these too and that they are not faults. See why Stoicism is not about suppressing emotions.
  2. The judgment. I have been wronged, and this must be answered.
  3. The assent. You agree. Now it is anger, and now it is running you.

The gap between one and three is where every remedy operates. Once you are through it, Seneca says, there is no steering; you can only wait it out and clean up.

Seneca’s remedies

Delay. The best of them, and the one he returns to most. The greatest remedy for anger is postponement: ask it to wait, not so that it will forgive, but so that it can judge. Anger’s whole power depends on immediacy. Almost nothing survives a genuine pause.

Check the facts first. A great deal of anger rests on something not yet established: that the slight was intended, that the report is accurate, that you understood correctly. Seneca notes how often we are furious about an account we have not verified.

Consider the offender. Were they ignorant? Tired? Repeating what was done to them? Marcus makes this the opening move of the entire Meditations: you will meet the meddling and the ungrateful today, and they are like that because they cannot tell good from evil.

Consider yourself. Seneca’s most uncomfortable remedy. You have done this. Perhaps not today, perhaps not to this person, but the thing you are furious about is on your own record somewhere. Anger depends on a distinction between you and them that mostly does not survive inspection.

Expect it in advance. Most anger contains surprise, and surprise is optional. This is premeditatio malorum applied to people: if you have already granted that traffic exists and colleagues are careless, their being so is not an outrage.

Marcus’s list

Meditations XI.18 is the most concrete passage on anger anywhere in Stoic literature: ten considerations for when someone has wronged you. Among them, in compressed form:

  • We are made for one another, and to obstruct each other is against nature
  • Consider what they believed they were doing; almost nobody acts believing they are wrong
  • You have done the same
  • You cannot be certain of their intent
  • Your anger is doing more to you than their offense did
  • Neither of you will be here long

And then the one he clearly needed most, and which reads like a man catching himself mid-thought: the offense exists in your judgment of it. Remove the judgment and the injury goes with it.

Why anger is hard to give up

Because it feels like integrity.

Anger arrives dressed as moral seriousness. It presents itself as the proof that you care about the thing being violated, so letting it go feels like consenting. This is the trap Seneca is trying to describe: the sensation of righteousness is not evidence that the anger is useful. You can hold that something is genuinely wrong, and act to correct it, without the frenzy — and you will correct it better, because the frenzy wants punishment rather than repair.

The Stoics are not asking you to accept injustice. They are noting that anger is a poor instrument for fixing it.

Practicing it

In the morning, name where you will most likely be provoked today. Naming it in advance removes most of its ambush.

In the moment, the only reliable move is delay. Not suppression, not counting to ten as a trick, but genuinely declining to act while the state persists. Seneca says anger’s worst feature is that it will not be governed once accepted; the corollary is that everything depends on the seconds before you accept it.

In the evening, the examination is where the pattern shows. Almost everyone is angry about the same two or three things repeatedly, and that is invisible from inside any single day.

Further reading

Seneca’s On Anger, particularly Book III, which is the practical one. Marcus, Meditations XI.18 and II.1. Both are on our Library, and which translation makes more difference than you would expect.
