Is Stoicism a Religion?

By Gio White·How sources are checked

No. Stoicism has no worship, no revelation, no clergy, no scripture, no salvation and no meaningful doctrine of an afterlife. It is a philosophy, and it was taught in schools alongside rival philosophies that disagreed with it.

But the honest answer does not stop there, because the people who say "Stoicism is just practical philosophy, the god stuff is decoration" are describing modern Stoicism, not the ancient article.

The ancient Stoics were theists

Not in a vague way. Stoic physics held that the cosmos is a single living, rational being, ordered throughout by an active principle they called logos, and also called Zeus, and also called nature and fate and providence, treating those as names for the same thing. Everything that happens follows necessarily from that order, and the order is good.

This is not window dressing on the ethics. It is the foundation of it. Live according to nature means live in accordance with that rational order, and the reason acceptance makes sense is that what happens is the working-out of something intelligent rather than the churn of something indifferent.

Cleanthes, the second head of the school, wrote a Hymn to Zeus that is a genuine religious poem. Epictetus talks about god constantly, tells his students they are fragments of god, and frames the whole practice as playing the role assigned to you by a benevolent director.

If you strip that out you are left with something real and usable, but you should know you have removed a load-bearing wall and not merely redecorated.

Marcus and the gods-or-atoms move

Marcus Aurelius does something more interesting, and it is the most useful passage in this whole discussion.

Repeatedly, in several books, he sets out a disjunction: either the universe is providence, or it is atoms and chance. Either there is an ordering intelligence, or there is not. He does not settle it. What he does instead is show that his conclusions hold on both branches.

If providence, then what happens is assigned to you and your job is to meet it well. If atoms, then nothing is aimed at you, there is no one to complain to, and your job is still to meet it well. Either way, the only thing in your possession is how you respond, and the case for acting justly does not change.

This is the honest bridge for a secular reader, and it comes from a Stoic emperor rather than from a modern apologist. He is not being coy. He is noticing that the ethics survive the metaphysical uncertainty.

Can you practice it without the theology?

Most modern Stoics do, and there is a real argument about whether that is legitimate.

The traditionalist objection is that removing providence turns "live according to nature" into a slogan with no content, and turns acceptance into mere resignation, since accepting the deliverances of a rational order is a different act from accepting the output of blind chance.

The modern reply, and Marcus is its best evidence, is that the practical core stands on its own: the dichotomy of control does not require a benevolent cosmos, the four virtues do not require a divine legislator, and the exercises work as psychological technique regardless of what is underneath.

Both are defensible. What is not defensible is pretending the question does not exist, which is what most popular Stoicism does.

Is it compatible with a religion you already have?

Generally yes, and historically this is well attested. Early Christian writers borrowed heavily from Stoic ethics, sometimes silently. Stoic practice has been taken up by Christians, Jews, Muslims and Buddhists without much friction, because most of the material is about conduct and attention rather than about ultimate claims.

The points of real tension, if you want them:

  • Stoic determinism sits awkwardly with strong accounts of free will
  • Stoic providence identifies god with the cosmos rather than placing him outside it, which is pantheism and unacceptable to most monotheisms as stated
  • The Stoic sage needs nothing external, which sits oddly with traditions built on dependence or grace

In practice most people take the ethics and the exercises and leave the physics, which is exactly what the modern secular practitioner does from the other direction.

The short version

It is a philosophy, not a religion. The ancient version was religious in a way that modern practice mostly is not. Marcus, who had both options in front of him, decided the practice held either way and got on with the day.

That is probably the right posture for most people arriving now.

Further reading

Meditations IV, IX and XII contain the gods-or-atoms passages, and they are short. Epictetus’s Discourses are the most explicitly theistic of the three main sources. Both are on our Library.

For the practice itself, which is where most of the value is regardless of where you land here, start with how to keep a Stoic journal or the dichotomy of control.
