How to Actually Read the Meditations

By Gio White·How sources are checked

Most people who bounce off the Meditations do so for a structural reason rather than a philosophical one. They open it expecting a book and find something else.

It is not a book. It is a private notebook, written in Greek, on campaign, by a man with no intention of being read. It has no thesis, no progression, no chapters in any meaningful sense, and no editor. The division into twelve books is not his. The order is not his either. Read it the way you would read an argument and it will seem repetitive and disorganized, because it is repetitive and disorganized, and both on purpose.

Here is how to read it so it works.

Skip Book One the first time

Book One is a list of debts: what Marcus learned from his grandfather, his mother, his tutors, his adoptive father. Roughly sixty short entries thanking people you have never heard of for qualities described in the abstract.

It is genuinely moving once you know him, and it is the single worst place to begin. Most abandoned copies of the Meditations are abandoned in Book One.

Start at Book Two. It opens at dawn with him telling himself he is about to meet the meddling, the ungrateful and the arrogant. That is the actual voice of the book. Come back to Book One at the end, when you will recognize what he is grateful for.

Read it in fragments

The entries are numbered and mostly short, and they do not build. Nothing in II.4 depends on II.3. This means you can read one paragraph and stop, which is how the book was made and how it works best.

A page a day is a better dose than an hour a week. Several people who love it have never read it cover to cover in a sitting, and there is no reason to.

Expect the repetition, and read it as the point

Marcus tells himself the same handful of things over and over. People will be difficult. You will die. Nothing outside your judgment is yours. Do the work in front of you. Do not waste time on what others say.

Read as a book, that is a defect. Read as what it is — a man doing reps — it is the most useful thing about it. He is not developing an argument he expects to finish. He is returning to the same few positions because they keep failing to hold, which is what practice looks like from the inside. If your own journal starts repeating after a month, this is your evidence that nothing has gone wrong.

Mark what recurs, not what impresses

The temptation is to highlight the aphorisms. The more useful exercise is to notice which ideas he returns to most, because those are the ones he needed most, and the pattern is a portrait.

Count them and you find: mortality, other people’s failings, the worthlessness of posthumous fame, the irrelevance of reputation, and the instruction to get up and do the day’s work. That is a man with a specific set of temptations — vanity, irritation, self-pity, staying in bed — and the book is him managing them for twenty years.

Do not read it for quotations

Two reasons.

The translations differ so widely that the same passage appears in wordings sharing almost no vocabulary, which makes quoting from memory unreliable. And this is the single most misattributed text in popular circulation: lines from films, songs and modern self-help are routinely printed under Marcus’s name. We keep a list, including the Gladiator line that he not only did not write but argued against.

If you want to quote him, quote with a book and chapter number from an edition you own.

Which edition, briefly

Hays for reading, Hard for keeping, and the free version is George Long’s 1862 English, which is why it sounds Victorian. The longer version of that argument is in which translation of Meditations should you read.

A reading plan that works

  1. Book Two, whole, in one sitting. About fifteen minutes.
  2. Books Three through Six, a page a day. This is the core.
  3. Stop when it stops landing. It is not a text you owe anything to.
  4. Book One, last, when the names mean something.
  5. Then reread Book Two. It reads differently, and that difference is the whole point of having read the rest.

Books Seven through Twelve are more fragmentary and repeat the earlier material. Worth having; not worth pushing through in order.
