Who Said "Be Kind, for Everyone You Meet Is Fighting a Hard Battle"?

By Gio White·How sources are checked

Not Seneca. Not Plato, and not Philo of Alexandria, who both get the credit about as often.

It was written by Ian Maclaren, the pen name of John Watson, a Scottish Free Church minister and a very popular novelist in his day, in a Christmas message printed in December 1897. He is remembered now, if at all, for sentimental fiction about village life. He is quoted daily by people who have never heard of him.

And he did not write the sentence you know either.

What he actually wrote

The 1897 wording was:

Be pitiful, for every man is fighting a hard battle.

Every part of that has since been edited except the battle.

"Be pitiful" became "be kind." "Every man" became "everyone you meet." The result is smoother, more inclusive, and considerably easier to put on a card, and not one of those changes was made by Maclaren, who was dead by 1907.

The word that had to go

The substitution worth noticing is the first one, because it was not a stylistic preference. It was a repair.

In Victorian English, pitiful was an active word. It described a person who was full of pity, who felt for others. To be pitiful was a virtue and a fairly demanding one. That sense is now dead. The word inverted, and it now describes the object rather than the subject: someone contemptible, arousing pity rather than extending it.

So the original sentence stopped working. Read today, "be pitiful" instructs you to be pathetic. The line survived only because someone, at some point, quietly swapped in a word that still meant what Maclaren meant.

This is the part I find genuinely interesting. The quotation is not a fixed object that got misfiled. It is a sentence that has been maintained, by anonymous hands, for a hundred and twenty years, and each repair made it a little less Victorian.

Which is exactly why it drifted to antiquity

Here is the mechanism, and it explains a large share of our misattributions list.

A sentence carries markers of when it was written. Diction, rhythm, the words that have since shifted. Those markers are what let a reader place it. Strip them out, and the sentence becomes undatable. "Be kind, for everyone you meet is fighting a hard battle" contains nothing that fixes it to any century. It could be from anywhere.

An undatable sentence that sounds wise does not stay anonymous for long. It gets assigned. And it gets assigned upward, to whoever carries the most authority in the neighborhood of its subject. For a line about how to treat other people, that means Plato, or Philo, or Seneca.

Nobody decided to lie. The attribution filled a vacuum that the editing created.

Would Seneca have agreed with it?

Here the case gets awkward in a way the quote cards never mention.

Seneca wrote at length about this exact distinction, and he came down against pity. In De Clementia he separates clementia, clemency, which he counts a virtue, from misericordia, pity, which he treats as a fault: a disturbance of the mind brought on by the sight of someone else’s suffering. His position is that the wise person will do everything the pitying person does, will help, relieve, and give, but will not be shaken while doing it. Help without the ache.

So the sentence most often credited to Seneca contains, in its original wording, precisely the thing Seneca argued against.

Does that make it un-Stoic?

No, and the reason is more interesting than a simple yes or no.

The modern rewording accidentally moved the line onto Stoic ground. Kindness is not pity. Stoicism has a strong account of what you owe other people: oikeiōsis, the widening of concern outward from yourself to family, to community, to the whole of humankind, and justice as the virtue that is entirely about other people. Marcus opens his day by reminding himself that he will meet the ungrateful and the arrogant, and concludes that he cannot be harmed by them and must not hate them. That is not far from Maclaren’s point.

What Stoicism resists is the idea that you must suffer alongside someone in order to help them, on the grounds that a second person in distress has not improved the situation.

So: "be kind, because everyone is carrying something" is a thoroughly Stoic sentence. "Be pitiful" is not, quite. The century of anonymous editing that made the line quotable also, by accident, made it more Stoic than it started out.

That is a much stranger thing to have happened than a simple misattribution, and it is the reason this one is worth more than a correction.

What to read instead

The passage on clemency and pity is in De Clementia, Book II, and it is short. It is one of the least sentimental things in Stoicism and one of the most practical, because it is about how to help people repeatedly without being destroyed by it.

For the argument about what you owe strangers, Marcus is the better entry point, and Book II of the Meditations opens on exactly this.
