The Four Stoic Virtues

By Gio White·How sources are checked

Ask a Stoic what a good life consists of and the answer is one word: virtue. Not virtue as a synonym for wholesomeness, but aretē, excellence, a thing performing its function well. A good knife cuts. A good human being reasons and acts well.

Zeno of Citium put the doctrine at the center of the school he founded on a public porch in Athens: virtue is the only good. Health, wealth, reputation and comfort are not evils, but they are not goods either. They are material, and what you do with them is the only part that counts.

That doctrine gets its shape from four virtues the Stoics inherited from Plato and made the frame of their entire ethics.

The four

Wisdom — phronēsis, practical wisdom.

The virtue of discernment: seeing things as they actually are rather than as you wish or fear them to be. It is not erudition. A very learned person can be catastrophically unwise, and a Stoic would say that combination is common. Wisdom is knowing what is true, what matters, and what is yours to do — which is why it is the virtue the other three depend on. Courage without wisdom is recklessness; justice without wisdom does harm while meaning well.

Courage — andreia.

Not fearlessness, and not aggression. The willingness to act rightly when it costs something. The Stoics extend it well past the battlefield: enduring illness, holding an unpopular position, having the conversation you have been avoiding, doing honest work when distraction is easier. Courage is required whenever you already know the right thing and feel resistance to doing it, which is most days.

Justice — dikaiosynē.

Giving people what they are owed, starting with honesty. This is the most socially demanding of the four and the one most often quietly dropped from modern Stoicism, which prefers the calm-under-pressure parts. It should not be dropped. Marcus Aurelius, who had more scope than most to be self-interested, keeps returning to the idea that we were made to work with one another, and that acting against another person is acting against yourself.

The Stoics grounded this in oikeiōsis, the widening of concern from oneself outward to family, community, and eventually all rational beings — the origin of the phrase "citizen of the world," which is Stoic before it is anything else.

Temperance — sōphrosynē.

Wanting in right measure. Neither indulgence nor deprivation but proportion: restraint as a sense of scale rather than as denial. It governs appetite, anger, ambition and speech. Of the four, it is the one that shows up in the smallest decisions and therefore gets practiced most often.

Where they come from, and why it matters

The four are not originally Stoic. They are Plato’s, set out in the Republic, and they were the common inheritance of Greek ethics before Zeno adopted them.

What the Stoics did was make them unified and sufficient. Unified in that they held you cannot really have one without the others — they are aspects of a single excellence rather than a menu. And sufficient in that virtue alone is enough for a good life, with no minimum quantity of health or fortune required underneath it. That second claim is the hardest thing the Stoics ever asserted, and the one their ancient critics attacked most.

You do not have to accept the strong version to use the framework. Most modern practitioners hold a softer position: virtue is what matters most, and the rest is preferable but not decisive.

Using them as a review, not a checklist

The four make a poor to-do list and an excellent set of questions. The difference is the direction: a checklist asks whether you were good, which invites a yes. A review asks where, which requires evidence.

Once a week, one question each, with a specific instance rather than a general impression:

  • Wisdom. Where did I see clearly this week, and where did I see what I wanted to see?
  • Courage. What did I do that cost something? What did I avoid because it would have?
  • Justice. Who did I owe something to, and did I pay it? Where was I less than honest?
  • Temperance. Where was I out of proportion — in appetite, in anger, in how much I said?

Two rules make this work. Name an instance, not a tendency: "I was patient" is unfalsifiable, "I did not interrupt her on Thursday" is a fact. And look for the pattern across weeks, because a single week is too short to show anything. Everyone has one virtue they are quietly strong in and one they consistently duck, and neither is visible from inside a single review.

Expect one of the four to be uncomfortable. That is the useful one.

The common misreading

Stoicism is frequently sold as emotional armour: stay calm, do not let things affect you, control your reactions. The four virtues make it obvious that this is at best a quarter of the thing.

Three of the four are about acting, and one of them is entirely about other people. A practice that produces a calmer person who is no more just, no more honest and no braver has not produced a Stoic. It has produced someone who is harder to bother — which the ancients would have regarded as closer to the Epicurean project, and not the point.

The test the Stoics would apply is not whether you feel less. It is whether you act better.
