## Cold open

[Gym-wall still, slow push in. Quote typed on screen.]

"What we do in life echoes in eternity." Marcus Aurelius.

It's on gym walls, on tattoos, on a thousand motivational posters.

He never said it.

[Hard cut to black. Title card.]

And he's not the only one. Seneca gets credit for song lyrics. Epictetus gets credit for self-help books he died eighteen centuries too early to read.

So today: ten famous Stoic quotes, where each one actually came from, and what the real Stoics wrote instead. At least one of these is probably on your wall. The full source trail for every one is linked in the description, so you don't have to take my word for any of it.

## 10. "Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity." (Seneca)

[Locker room, business book covers, a LinkedIn-style post blurred.]

You'll find this under Seneca's name in business books, locker rooms, and a very large number of LinkedIn posts.

Now go looking for it. Seneca left us one hundred and twenty-four letters, a dozen essays, and a shelf of tragedies. It isn't in any of them.

The line is twentieth-century American. Who said it first is genuinely disputed, so I won't pin it on anyone. But that's the first pattern on this list. When a good sentence has no author, a famous name moves in, because a famous name makes a sentence feel true.

## 9. "Circumstances do not make the man, they only reveal him to himself." (Epictetus)

[Ancient classroom, oil lamp. Then a small Edwardian book on a desk.]

Epictetus was born a slave in the first century and taught philosophy in Greece.

This sentence was published in 1903, in a short self-help book called As a Man Thinketh, by an English writer named James Allen.

Once you know that, you can hear it. The rhythm is Edwardian inspiration, not a Greek teacher working through an argument with his students.

The irony is that Epictetus had a lot to say about circumstances. His whole teaching begins by splitting the world in two: what is up to us, and what isn't. But he said it in his own words, and they're sharper than this one.

## 8. "Be kind, for everyone you meet is fighting a hard battle." (Seneca, sometimes Plato)

[Victorian study, snow outside, a letter being written.]

This one is lovely. It's also Victorian.

It traces to Ian Maclaren, the pen name of a Scottish minister named John Watson, writing a Christmas message in 1897.

You can see why it drifted toward the Stoics. They did think about difficult people, constantly. Here's how Marcus Aurelius opened one of his mornings:

[Text on screen, Meditations II.1, George Long translation.]

"Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil."

Less warm. More honest. And he goes on to say he can't be harmed by any of them, and that he was made to work alongside them anyway.

## 7. "One must cultivate one's garden." (Seneca)

[Garden at dusk, a figure with a spade, face not visible.]

This is the last line of Candide, by Voltaire, published in 1759.

And this one isn't just misattributed. It's backwards.

Candide is a satire of the belief that everything happens for the best, that the world is arranged by a wise providence. The Stoics believed exactly that. Voltaire's hero spends the whole book being battered by disasters, gives up on grand explanations, and goes home to work in his garden.

Putting Seneca's name on that line is like signing a critic's review with the name of the person being reviewed.

## 6. "Every new beginning comes from some other beginning's end." (Seneca)

[A bar at closing, chairs going up on tables, lights coming on.]

If this one sounds familiar and you can't place it, you've probably heard it at two in the morning.

It's from "Closing Time" by Semisonic, 1998, written by Dan Wilson. A song about a bar shutting for the night. Wilson has said he was also writing about his daughter being born.

It's a good line. It's just not Latin.

## 5. "When you arise in the morning, think of what a precious privilege it is to be alive, to breathe, to think, to enjoy, to love." (Marcus Aurelius)

[Sunrise over a city, then a 1910s printing press.]

This is one of the most shared Marcus Aurelius quotes there is. It was written in 1913, by Elbert Hubbard, an American publisher, in his magazine The Fra.

The tell is the tone. Marcus did write about mornings. Here's what he actually said:

[Text on screen, Meditations V.1, Gregory Hays translation.]

"At dawn, when you have trouble getting out of bed, tell yourself: I have to go to work, as a human being."

He isn't celebrating the morning. He's arguing with himself to get up. It's less pretty, and a lot more useful, because anyone who has ever hit snooze knows which of these two men is being honest.

## 4. "Everything we hear is an opinion, not a fact. Everything we see is a perspective, not the truth." (Marcus Aurelius)

[Feed of phone screens scrolling, then a single candle.]

If you follow any Stoic account, you've seen this one. No translation of the Meditations contains it.

Its closest ancestor is a tiny entry in book two, section fifteen, where Marcus quotes an earlier philosopher, Monimus the Cynic, to the effect that everything is what you suppose it to be. **VERIFY: II.15 wording in Hays; keep this as paraphrase, no quote marks.**

And the rewrite changes the meaning. Marcus is talking about judgment: the verdict you add on top of what happens. The viral version says your senses can't be trusted, which is not a Stoic position at all. The Stoics thought perception, handled carefully, could grasp the truth.

Credit where it's due: this one was identified by Thomas Colligan, writing for Modern Stoicism.

## 3. "It's not what happens to you, but how you react to it that matters." (Epictetus)

[A storm hitting a window, a figure seated inside, calm.]

Now we're getting close. This one is a modern paraphrase of something Epictetus really did say. Here's the original:

[Text on screen, Enchiridion 5.]

"Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things."

Notice the difference. The paraphrase is about reacting. Epictetus is about judging. For him, the opinion comes before the reaction, and the opinion is where the work happens. That distinction is the one modern cognitive therapy later built on.

So the paraphrase isn't wrong, exactly. It's just not a quote, and it loses the most useful part.

## 2. "The obstacle is the way." (Marcus Aurelius)

[A stone in a stream, water parting around it.]

This one's the closest of all. The thought really is his. Here's Meditations book five, section twenty:

[Text on screen, Meditations V.20, Gregory Hays translation.]

"The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way."

The five-word version is the title of Ryan Holiday's 2014 book, and Holiday is completely open about where it came from. What happened next is the internet dropped the book, kept the title, put it in quotation marks, and signed it Marcus Aurelius.

So: the right idea, the right man, the wrong words. And the real words are better.

## 1. "What we do in life echoes in eternity." (Marcus Aurelius)

[Back to the gym wall. Then a Roman field at dawn, armor, no faces.]

And the most famous of all.

This line is from the 2000 film Gladiator, spoken by Russell Crowe as Maximus. The screenplay is by David Franzoni, John Logan, and William Nicholson.

The film's emperor is Marcus Aurelius, and he's on screen in the opening act. That's how the line got attached to him. The most famous thing Marcus Aurelius ever "said" was written in Hollywood in the late 1990s, about eighteen hundred years after he died.

And here's the part I love. The real Marcus spent a lot of the Meditations telling himself that fame after death is worthless. In one entry, he sums up a human life in a list, and it ends with this:

[Text on screen, Meditations II.17.]

"Lasting fame: uncertain." **VERIFY: II.17 wording and translation; repo text reads "Lasting fame: uncertain." Confirm which translation.**

A man who wrote that doesn't go around announcing that his deeds will echo in eternity.

## Close

[Slow montage of the ten quotes, each stamped with its real source.]

So why do the fakes spread better than the real thing?

Because most of them were written to be quoted. They're smooth, they fit on a poster, and they flatter you. The real Stoics were writing to themselves, or to students, and it shows: they're blunter, stranger, and much more useful on a bad day.

If you want to spot a fake, there are three tells. The rhythm sounds modern. There's no book or section number anywhere near it. And it's a little too perfect.

The Meditations was a private journal. Marcus never meant anyone to read it, which is exactly why it's worth reading.
